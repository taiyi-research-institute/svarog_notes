# Revisiting DKLs Threshold ECDSA: Enhanced OT-based VOLE and Two-Party Signing

Gilad Asharov<sup>∗</sup> Utila

May 17, 2026

## Abstract

Threshold ECDSA signing has become a standard building block for securing cryptocurrency assets, with the protocol of Doerner, Kondi, Lee, and shelat (DKLs, IEEE S&amp;P 2024) emerging as a leading solution due to its efficiency and widespread industry adoption.
In this work, we revisit the DKLs protocol to evaluate its concrete security and implementation trade-offs:

- Vector Oblivious Linear Evaluation (VOLE): We identify subtle issues in the underlying OT-based Vector Oblivious Linear Evaluation (VOLE) sub-protocol, showing that original parameter choices must be adjusted to reach intended security levels.
To address this, we provide a complete analysis of three VOLE variants offering different trade-offs between bandwidth and round complexity.

- Two-Party Signing: We introduce an optimized two-party signing protocol that shifts the majority of computation and communication to a message- and key-independent pre-processing phase.
This results in an exceptionally efficient online phase where each party exchanges only 0.2KB, a roughly $600\times$ reduction in communication compared to the full protocol, without being susceptible to known “pre-signature” attacks.

Our findings consolidate the security of the protocol while providing significant efficiency improvements for practical deployment and standardization.

## 1 Introduction

ECDSA is the dominant signature scheme used to control cryptocurrency assets, securing transactions worth billions of dollars daily.
To mitigate single points of failure, modern systems increasingly rely on $t$-out-of-$n$ threshold ECDSA, where any subset of $t$ parties can jointly produce a valid signature, while no coalition of at most $t-1$ parties can do so, even when interacting with honest participants.
Threshold ECDSA has therefore become a standard building block in deployed cryptographic wallet infrastructures.

At a high level, the parties hold shares $\mathsf{sk}_{i}$ of a secret key $\mathsf{sk}$ corresponding to a public key $\mathsf{pk},$ and collaboratively compute a signature $\sigma =\mathsf{Sign}_{\mathsf{sk}}(m)$ on a message $m.$ Recent years have seen significant progress in designing efficient threshold ECDSA protocols (see, e.g., [Lin17, HLNR18, DKLs18, CGG+20, KU25] and references therein). Among these, the protocol of Doerner, Kondi, Lee, and shelat (DKLs) [DKLs24], building upon previous protocols by the same authors [DKLs18, DKLs19], stands out for its conceptual clarity, elegance, and practical efficiency.
It has been adopted in several real-world systems, including companies such as Coinbase, Silence Laboratories, Alore/0xCarbon, Taurus Group, Utila, Vultisig, Copper, Sodot, Web3auth, and more (see [DKL25]).

Our perspective and contributions.
Motivated by the increasing deployment of the DKLs protocol, we revisit its design and analysis with the goal of understanding its concrete security guarantees and implementation trade-offs in the plain model.
At a high level:

- OT-Based Multiplication (VOLE): We identify several subtle issues arising from the interaction between the protocol’s building blocks, most notably in the vector oblivious linear evaluation (VOLE) sub-protocol, which is instantiated from oblivious transfer (OT). Our analysis shows that the original parameter choices must be adjusted in order to fully meet the protocol’s intended security levels.
In particular, implementations that follow the protocol exactly as described in [DKLs24] may fail to achieve their targeted security guarantees.
Achieving the desired level of security requires either increased communication bandwidth or additional interaction rounds.
We provide a complete analysis of three protocol variants, each offering a different trade-offbetween bandwidth and round complexity.

- Fast Two-Party Signing: The two-party setting already captures the core threshold-signing model: the signing key is shared between two parties, so neither party can generate signatures on its own.
This distributes trust and removes any single point of key compromise.
This setting is also the focus of several wallet providers.
We therefore optimize the two-party signing protocol of [DKLs24].

Our starting observation is that several steps in the multiparty signing protocol of [DKLs24] become unnecessary in the two-party setting.
By eliminating these redundancies, we reduce both communication and round complexity, while still accounting for the additional interaction required by the strengthened underlying VOLE protocol.

We further show that a large fraction of the protocol’s interaction and computation can be shifted to a preprocessing phase.
This preprocessing produces an extremely small state of less than $0.$ 3KB. The subsequent online phase is highly efficient: each party sends and receives only about $0.$ 2KB, and performs less than 1% of the computation required in preprocessing.
This stands in sharp contrast to a direct execution of the full protocol, which requires more than $\approx 600\times$ as much communication, with each party sending approximately 200KB. Importantly, the preprocessing phase is independent of both the message $m$ and the secret key $\mathsf{sk},$ and the nonce is not revealed prior to choosing the message to be signed, thereby avoiding the known attacks on “pre-signatures” identified by Groth and Shoup [GS22].

Our work both consolidates the security of the protocol and suggests direct efficiency improvements.
Because this protocol is already deployed in systems securing substantial financial value, even subtle mismatches between theoretical analysis and practical implementation can have significant consequences.
Furthermore, the protocol is a strong candidate for standardization by NIST. We believe our work contributes to this effort by helping to formalize a highly secure, correctly parameterized instantiation.
More broadly, our analysis highlights several intriguing theoretical questions with direct implications for practical schemes, which may inspire further research.

## 2 Technical Overview

The construction of [DKLs24] suggests a three-round protocol for generating a signature.
However, realizing this round complexity in the plain model is non-trivial.
At a high level, the construction provides: (a) a threshold signing protocol given a multiplication protocol; and (b) an instantiation of a multiplication protocol given an oblivious transfer on random inputs.
We start with an overview of each component separately.

<a id="section-2-1-multiplication-oblivious-linear-evaluation"></a>

### 2.1 Multiplication (Oblivious Linear Evaluation)

In Vector Oblivious Linear Evaluation (VOLE), the Sender holds $(a_{1},...,a_{\ell })\in \mathbb{Z}_{q}^{\ell },$ while the Receiver holds $\beta \in \mathbb{Z}_{q},$ and the goal is to obtain additive shares of $(a_{1}\cdot \beta ,...,a_{\ell }\cdot \beta )$ over $\mathbb{Z}_{q}.$

VOLE can be viewed as an arithmetic analogue of oblivious transfer (OT). In OT, the Sender holds two strings $(x_{0},x_{1})$ and the Receiver holds a choice bit $\sigma ,$ learning only $x_{\sigma}.$ VOLE replaces this binary selection with a linear correlation over a field.
The construction of [DKLs24] realizes VOLE using OT as a subroutine with random inputs, where the Receiver’s value $\beta$ is (implicitly) sampled uniformly by the protocol.
Thus, implementing VOLE reduces to instantiating OT on random inputs.

Several such random OT variants have been studied, including Sender Random OT, Random Receiver OT [ALSZ17], and Endemic OT [MR19]. Among these, Endemic OT offers the most flexible abstraction and admits a single-round realization.
Moreover, the number of OT instances required in [DKLs24] is relatively small (e.g., about 376 for a statistical security parameter of 60). Hence, despite the reliance on public-key operations, directly using Endemic OT may be preferable to OT extension techniques, which introduce an additional round of interaction.
In particular, one-round Endemic OT yields a two-round VOLE protocol, consequently enabling a three-round signing protocol.

However, a subtle issue in [DKLs24] complicates this picture.
We observe that the suggested number of OTs does not suffice for the claimed security level.
Our analysis indicates that the number of required OTs must be increased significantly (from roughly 376 to approximately 1400). This leads to roughly a $4\times$ increase in communication, with each signature requiring at least 400KB of bandwidth.
Moreover, directly instantiating this many OTs using public-key cryptography entails substantial computational overhead, complicating direct implementations via Endemic OT.

On the number of oblivious transfers needed.
To detail why the required number of OTs must be increased, we review how the random input $\beta \in \mathbb{Z}_{q}$ of the VOLE is sampled in [DKLs24]:

The two parties sample a “gadget vector” $\mathbf{g}\in \mathbb{Z}_{q}^{\mathsf{m}}$ at the outset of the computation, derived from shared randomness, where $\mathsf{m}$ is the number of OTs.
The Receiver samples $\mathbf{w}\in \{0,1\}^{\mathsf{m}}$ as their random input to the oblivious transfer.
The input $\beta \in \mathbb{Z}_{q}$ is then derived as $\sum_{i=1}^{\mathsf{m}}g_{i}\cdot w_{i}.$ The vector $\mathbf{g}$ can be viewed as a hash function mapping $\{0,1\}^{\mathsf{m}}\rightarrow \mathbb{Z}_{q},$ denoted as $\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{w}).$

The leftover hash lemma [ILL89] guarantees that if (1) a random hash function $\mathbf{g}:\{0,1\}^{\mathsf{m}}\rightarrow$ $\mathsf{Scalar}$ is sampled from a universal family, and $(2)\mathbf{w}\leftarrow \{0,1\}^{\mathsf{m}}$ is drawn independently from a source $W$ with sufficiently high min-entropy, then $\beta =\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{w})$ is close to uniform over $\mathbb{Z}_{q}.$ The exact parameters of the lemma determine the required min-entropy of $W,$ which in turn dictates the number of oblivious transfers $\mathsf{m}$ (directly impacting communication complexity and efficiency). Crucially, $\mathbf{g}$ and $\mathbf{w}$ must be chosen independently.

The analysis of [DKLs24] treats $\mathbf{g}$ and $\mathbf{w}$ as independent and applies the leftover hash lemma<sup>1</sup> to deduce that $\beta$ is $2^{-\mathsf{s}}$-close to uniform when $\mathsf{m}\geq$ log $q+2\mathsf{s}$ for a statistical security parameter $\mathsf{s}.$ However, in the actual protocol, the setting differs subtly: Because $\mathbf{g}$ is chosen at the outset of the computation, an adversarial Sender can leverage this to carefully restrict the valid choices of $\mathbf{w}$ (assuming no abort occurs in the consistency check phase). In other words, the adversary can adaptively choose which min-entropy source $W$ over $\{0,1\}^{\mathsf{m}}$ to use after observing $\mathbf{g}.$

While the leftover hash lemma guarantees that a random $\mathbf{g}$ behaves well for any fixed high-min-entropy source, it does not rule out the possibility that for a fixed $\mathbf{g},$ there exists an adversarial chosen high-min-entropy source $\tilde{W}$ for which $\mathbf{g}(\tilde{W})$ is not sufficiently close to uniform.

We demonstrate that the original proof does not fully encompass this adaptive strategy under the proposed parameters.
To validate this, we conduct an experiment (detailed in Appendix A) using small parameters to test whether the condition $\mathsf{m}\geq$ log $q+2\cdot \mathsf{s}$ is sufficient.
By quantifying over all possible $\mathbf{g}$ and sources $W,$ our results indicate that there indeed exist specific $\mathbf g$’s that admit “bad” sources $W,$ causing the statistical distance to exceed the theoretical bound.
This confirms that the initial parameter choice requires adjustment.

Three variants.
Motivated by this observation, we propose three robust alternatives for the protocol instantiation.

Variant I: Standard Leftover Hash Lemma.
We begin by slightly modifying the protocol to fit cleanly into the standard setting of the leftover hash lemma, ensuring the hash function $\mathbf{g}$ and the source $W$ are chosen independently.
We provide this variant primarily for pedagogical purposes to aid understanding.
Because the randomness of $\beta =\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{w})$ is meant to protect against an adversarial Sender, we can simply have the Receiver pick $\mathbf{g}$ uniformly at random at the end of the protocol and send it to the Sender.
With this adjustment, the leftover hash lemma applies directly, allowing for small parameters: $\mathsf{m}\geq$ log $q+3\cdot \mathsf{s}$ (roughly 436 OTs for $\mathsf{s}=60).$

However, this variant introduces two practical trade-offs: (1) It adds an extra communication round (to send $\mathbf{g}),$ and (2) the Receiver obtains the value $b=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{w})$ only after receiving the second message, rather than at the end of the first round.
Consequently, $\beta$ is only available to the outer protocol at the very end of the VOLE execution, see a discussion on that below.

Variant II: Leftover Hash Lemma with Mildly Adversarial Hash Choice.
To avoid adding an extra message, we explore a Fiat-Shamir-like transformation.
In this approach, we derive $\mathbf{g}$ by applying a random oracle to the final message from the Sender.
This means $\mathbf{g}$ is based (indirectly) on the source $W$ already fixed by the Sender.

<sup>1</sup>In [DKLs24], the lemma is attributed to Proposition 1.1 of Impagliazzo and Naor [IN96].

This approach provides slightly more power to an adversarial Sender compared to Variant I. The Sender could theoretically test multiple candidate sources $W,$ observe the resulting hashes $\mathbf{g},$ and select the most advantageous combination.
We show that this extra power can be safely mitigated by increasing the number of OTs by an additive factor of $2\kappa ,$ where $\kappa$ is the computational security parameter (typically 128). This requires $\mathsf{m}\geq$ log $q+3\cdot \mathsf{s}+2\cdot \kappa .$ Concretely, for $\mathsf{s}=60$ and $\kappa =128,$ this totals 692 OTs (rounded in implementations to 696 to be a multiple of 8).

While this approach preserves the original round complexity of the VOLE, the Receiver still learns $\beta$ late in the protocol.
Because the signing protocol originally requires $\beta$ at the end of the first VOLE round, directly plugging in this variant would add a round to the signing protocol.
However, as we will demonstrate in Section 6, we can modify the two-round signing protocol so that $\beta$ is not needed early on, making it fully compatible with this VOLE variant.

Variant III: Leftover Hash Lemma with Adversarial Source-Fixing.
The third variant aligns exactly with the security game of the original [DKLs24] protocol, where $\mathbf{g}$ is sampled first, and the adversary subsequently chooses a $\mathbf{g}$-dependent high-min-entropy source $W.$ We prove that, provided the number of OTs is sufficiently large, the vast majority of hash functions $\mathbf{g}$ are “good”—meaning that for every high-min-entropy choice of $W,$ the distribution $\mathbf{g}(W)$ remains close to uniform.
Achieving this guarantee, however, requires satisfying $\mathsf{m}\geq$ log $q+O(\mathsf{s}\cdot$ log $\mathsf{m}).$ This results in the previously mentioned $\approx 1400$ OTs.

We emphasize that our analysis here is conservative.
We apply the leftover hash lemma directly, without overly optimizing constants or relying on complex bounds.
Our analysis is intentionally simple: it relies on the leftover hash lemma and a basic union bound.
We believe that in practice, transparent and easily verifiable analysis is often preferable for high-assurance systems, even if it yields slightly larger parameters.

Analysis.
The adversarial source-fixing issue also affects previous versions of the protocol [DKLs18, DKLs19]. We provide full, rigorous security proofs for all the three variants we mentioned above.
For clarity, our analysis first applies to a protocol with extra communication rounds (Variant I) to distill the exact properties needed, and we then apply a round-compressing transformation.
Our proof is naturally based on that of [DKLs24], but has a somewhat different structure.
For instance, while [DKLs24] has to rely on the leftover hash lemma in three different places, we apply it only once.

Implications.
Increasing the number of OTs has important implications for practical deployments.
With a larger number of OTs, it is natural to use OT extension protocols, which reduce the reliance on expensive public-key operations to a small number of base OTs (typically on the order of the computational security parameter, e.g., 128 or 256). From these, the necessary $\approx 1400$ additional OTs can be generated using inexpensive symmetric-key operations.

In particular, random OT extension protocols [ALSZ17, KOS15, Roy22] can be realized in a single round, assuming an initial setup phase that establishes the base OTs.
As observed in [DKLs24], these base OTs are reusable and can therefore be amortized as part of a preprocessing phase, such as distributed key generation.
Consequently, the overall round complexity can, in principle, remain at three rounds per signature when such a setup is available.

Unfortunately (and as noted in [vdP23]), even when executed honestly, OT extension protocols may leak information about the secret inputs of the base OTs, which are intended to remain hidden.
Moreover, in the event of an abort, the corresponding base OTs can no longer be safely reused.
This issue becomes particularly problematic when the setup is treated as immutable key material and shared across multiple concurrent signing sessions (say, where a key is split between independent pairs of servers to increase throughput). Halting all concurrent executions can be very difficult, and it may be difficult to propagate information about an abort in one instance quickly enough to prevent unsafe reuse in parallel instances.

To address this engineering challenge, the safest solution is to assume no shared state between instances (rather than the key material) and re-execute the base OTs independently for each signature.
While this approach avoids the risks associated with reuse, it comes at the cost of additional communication and increased round complexity.
The increased communication and computation due to base OTs, and an additional round due to OT extension, motivates us to revisit the signing protocol and look for further optimizations, while focusing on two-party signing as it already captures the core threshold-signing model.

### 2.2 Two-Party Signing

Before proceeding to the threshold signing protocol, we briefly recall the standard ECDSA signature scheme and the approach of [DKLs24]. An ECDSA signature requires the following steps:

1. Choose a uniform $k\leftarrow \mathbb{Z}_{q},$ and let $R=k\cdot G.$

2. Let $r_{\mathsf{x}}$ be the $x$-coordinate of $R.$

3. Compute $s=k^{-1}\cdot (\mathsf{H}(m)+\mathsf{sk}\cdot r_{\mathsf{x}}),$ where $\mathsf{H}$ is a hash function.

4. Output $\sigma =(r_{\mathsf{x}},s).$

Overview of the DKLs24 signing protocol.
We simplify the overview for the two-party setting.
The protocol is symmetric; we provide the perspective of $P_{i}$ (ignoring additional OT rounds for brevity):

1. Phase I: Each party $P_{i}$ chooses a random $k_{i}\leftarrow \mathbb{Z}_{q},$ computes $R_{i}=k_{i}\cdot G,$ and commits to $R_{i}.$ The parties run two parallel VOLE sessions: one where $P_{i}$ is the Receiver and one where $P_{i}$ is the Sender.
At the end of this round, $P_{i}$ learns the random input $\beta _{i}\in \mathbb{Z}_{q}$ from the instance where it acted as the Receiver.

2. Phase II: The parallel VOLE instances conclude:

(a) In the first execution, $P_{0}$ (as Sender) inputs its share of the nonce $k_{0}$ and its share $\mathsf{sk}_{0}.$ The Receiver obtains the random masking $\beta _{1},$ yielding random additive shares of $k_{0}\cdot \beta _{1}$ and $\mathsf{sk}_{0}\cdot \beta _{1}.$

(b) In the second execution, $P_{1}$ (as Sender) inputs its shares, yielding additive shares of $k_{1}\cdot \beta _{0}$ and $\mathsf{sk}_{1}\cdot \beta _{0}.$

The parties also exchange decommitment information for $R_{0},R_{1},$ alongside verification data (which we omit in this overview) proving that their VOLE inputs $(k_{i},\mathsf{sk}_{i})$ are consistent with the public values $(\mathsf{pk}_{i},R_{i}).$ We denote the additive shares held by $P_{i}$ as $([[k_{0}\cdot \beta _{1}]]_{i},[[\mathsf{sk}_{0}\cdot \beta _{1}]]_{i}),$ and $([[k_{1}\cdot \beta _{0}]]_{i},[[\mathsf{sk}_{1}\cdot \beta _{0}]]_{i}).$

3. Phase III: The parties compute the joint nonce $R=R_{0}+R_{1},$ corresponding to $k=k_{0}+k_{1}$ (where $R=k\cdot G).$ They verify the VOLE shares against $R,\mathsf{pk}_{0},\mathsf{pk}_{1}.$ Then:

(a) $P_{0}$ knows $k_{0},\beta _{0}$ and holds shares of $k_{1}\cdot \beta _{0}$ and $k_{0}\cdot \beta _{1}.$ Respectively, $P_{1}$ knows $k_{1},\beta _{1}$ and holds shares to the same multiplications.
Using these, $P_{0}$ and $P_{1}$ compute $u_{0}$ and $u_{1}$ respectively, where:

$$
\begin{aligned}{u_{0}}&{{}= k_{0}\cdot \beta_{0}+ \lbrack\!\lbrack  k_{1}\cdot \beta_{0}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{0}+ \lbrack\!\lbrack  k_{0}\cdot \beta_{1}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{0},}\\{u_{1}}&{{}= k_{1}\cdot \beta_{1}+ \lbrack\!\lbrack  k_{1}\cdot \beta_{0}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{1}+ \lbrack\!\lbrack  k_{0}\cdot \beta_{1}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{1}.}\\ \end{aligned}
$$

Letting $\beta =\beta _{0}+\beta _{1},$ it holds that $u:=u_{0}+u_{1}=(k_{0}+k_{1})\cdot (\beta _{0}+\beta _{1})=k\cdot \beta .$

(b) In a similar manner, the parties compute $v_{0},v_{1},$ which are additive shares of $v:=\mathsf{sk}\cdot \beta .$

(c) Each party derives $r_{\mathsf{x}}$ from $R$ and computes:

$$
\begin{aligned}{w_{0}}&{{}= \mathsf{H}( m ) \cdot \beta_{0}+ r_{\mathsf{x}}\cdot v_{0},}\\{w_{1}}&{{}= \mathsf{H}( m ) \cdot \beta_{1}+ r_{\mathsf{x}}\cdot v_{1}.}\\ \end{aligned}
$$

It holds that $w:=w_{0}+w_{1}=\mathsf{H}(m)\cdot \beta +r_{\mathsf{x}}\cdot \mathsf{sk}\cdot \beta .$ The parties exchange $(u_{i},w_{i}),$ compute $(u,w)$ to obtain $s=w/u,$ and output signature $\sigma =(r_{\mathsf{x}},s).$

The multiparty case.
The original [DKLs24] protocol is designed for $n$ parties.
Each pair $(P_{i},P_{j})$ runs two instances of VOLE protocol, one in which $P_{i}$ is the Sender and one in which $P_{i}$ is the Receiver.
Therefore, two random maskings $\beta _{i,j}$ and $\beta _{j,i}$ are generated.
Each party $P_{i}$ has to correlate all the instances for which it acts as a Receiver to a global masking $\beta _{i}.$ Thus, $P_{i}$ must send shifts $(\beta _{i}-\beta _{i,j})_{j\not =i}$ during Phase II. Therefore, it is crucial that $\beta _{i,j}$ is learned in Phase I. This requirement creates friction if one wishes to use our Variant II, unless adding a round.
We emphasize that in the two-party case, this inter-instance correlation is unnecessary, meaning $\beta _{i}$ can safely be learned later in the protocol.
Furthermore, we show that the multiparty requirement to invoke an $F_{\mathsf{zero}}$ functionality (to provide shares of 0), which are necessary to hide the shares of the $\mathsf{sk},$ is unnecessary in the two-party setting.

Our modifications.
As discussed, Phase I requires multiple rounds to establish base OTs, and the VOLE itself demands high bandwidth.
To improve online efficiency, we push the bulk of the computation into an offline preprocessing phase that is completely independent of the message $m$ and the secret key $\mathsf{sk}.$

The VOLE protocol of [DKLs24] is not fully random – the inputs of the Sender are $k_{i}$ and $\mathsf{sk}_{i},$ while the “input” of the Receiver is random and is determined by the protocol.
In contrast, we modify the protocol to operate on completely random inputs during preprocessing.
Later, during the online signing phase, the parties send “corrections” to align the random VOLE inputs with their actual values.

Our optimized approach operates as follows:

1. Preprocessing: The parties choose nonces $k_{0},k_{1},$ commit to $R_{0},R_{1},$ and execute a completely random VOLE (including all necessary OTs). Note that the number of OTs is at least a few hundred.
At the end of the VOLE protocol, the state of each party is extremely small: a commitment to $R_{1-i},$ their own $k_{i}$ and its opening, four additive shares from the multiplications (i.e., four field elements), and their masking $\beta _{i}.$

2. Signing phase: Executed only when the parties agree to sign on a shared message $m.$ The key material is also only needed at this point.
The signing phase consists of two rounds:

(a) Round I: Now knowing $(k_{i},\mathsf{sk}_{i}),$ the parties adjust the random VOLE by sending scalar corrections.
Simultaneously, they complete Phase II of the original protocol: opening commitments for $R_{0},R_{1}$ and proving the verification information that they indeed use $k_{i}$ and $\mathsf{sk}_{i}.$

(b) Round II: Identical to the final round of [DKLs24]. The parties verify the multiplications, exchange $(u_{i},w_{i}),$ and compute and verify the signature $\sigma =(r_{\mathsf{x}},s).$

Comparison to [DKLs24, CGG+20]. Our preprocessing phase differs from what is considered in the literature as “pre-signatures” [GG20, CMP20, DJN+20, CGG+20] in two dimensions:

- The nonce is determined, but not revealed: Previous preprocessing [GG20, CMP20, DJN+20, CGG+20] required a single round (as opposed to two rounds by our approach). However, they revealed the public nonce $R$ to the parties before the message $m$ was determined.
This allows the adversary to choose the message $m$ as a function of $R,$ as pointed out by Groth and Shoup [GS22], which degrades security.
Because $R$ remains hidden in our preprocessing phase, our scheme maintains standard security guarantees (see [GS22]).

- Independence of the secret key $\mathsf{sk}.$ Our preprocessing is also independent of the secret key $\mathsf{sk}.$ This property is particularly useful for wallets that derive multiple addresses from the same source key material, for example, via hierarchical derivation methods such as BIP- 32 [Wui12]. Since preprocessing does not depend on $\mathsf{sk},$ the two devices can prepare signing information before the specific account or derived key is even selected.
Other pre-signatures depend on the shares of $\mathsf{sk}.$

We emphasize that our preprocessing is non-reusable and must be consumed per signature.
Furthermore, the entire communication and computation of the online phase is small.
The state each party has to store is roughly $0.$ 3KB, and each party has to send roughly $0.$ 2KB in the online phase.
By contrast, the offline phase, which requires sending $\approx$ 200KB (the exact communication depends on which variant is used).

We note that the two-party, two-message approach of [DKLs24] must still essentially execute the expensive VOLE and OT phases.
Moreover, this two-message protocol assumes that the base OTs can be reused, which, as discussed above, requires additional care.
If the base OTs are instead recomputed for each execution, then the protocol can no longer be implemented in two rounds.

Analysis.
We prove the security of our signing protocol by assuming the parties’ inputs are $\mathsf{valid}\mathsf{and}\mathsf{consistent},$ following the methodology established for Schnorr signatures [Lin24]. This formulation ensures the simulator knows the adversary’s share, as only one valid share exists given the public keys, and it was extracted during the key-generation.
While this requires specific, compatible key-generation procedures [Lin24, DKLs18] (and is not directly applied to the relaxed key-generation procedure of [DKLs24]), compatible key-generation procedures are highly efficient and widely deployed in practice.

<a id="section-2-3-an-efficient-attack-in-the-endemic-ot-hybrid-model"></a>

### 2.3 An Efficient Attack in the Endemic OT Hybrid Model

We conclude our technical overview by highlighting an important consideration for implementing the [DKLs24] VOLE protocol.
The protocol was proven secure by [DKLs24] in the Endemic OT-hybrid model [MR19]. Endemic OT picks uniform inputs for an honest Sender but allows an adversarial Sender to choose its own inputs.
The protocol relies on a challenge $\chi$ for consistency checks, derived via a Fiat-Shamir-like transformation applied to a portion of the Sender’s message.

We show that if the protocol is implemented exactly as abstracted in the hybrid model, an adversarial Sender can completely bypass the consistency check, breaking correctness in polynomial time.
The adversary achieves this by deriving the challenge first and subsequently tailoring its chosen OT inputs to perfectly cancel out the challenge (see Section 4.5 for technical details). At first glance, this resembles a standard Fiat-Shamir vulnerability where insufficient data was fed into the random oracle (e.g., [BPW12, HLPT20]). The standard fix is to hash the entire transcript.
However, because the hybrid model abstracts away the OT interaction, there is no formal “OT transcript” to hash that definitively binds the Sender’s inputs.

To resolve this in practice, implementers must hash the entire concrete transcript of the instantiated OT. Because some implementations might overlook this nuance when translating from the hybrid model to code (and we observed a few implementations that did overlook this nuance), we aim to raise awareness of this specific attack vector.
Furthermore, we prove that if one utilizes Sender Random OT [ALSZ17], which strictly enforces random inputs even for a malicious Sender, the protocol remains completely secure without requiring transcript hashing.

<a id="section-2-4-summary-of-our-contributions"></a>

### 2.4 Summary of Our Contributions

We summarize our contributions as follows:

1. We identify theoretical subtleties in the security analysis of [DKLs24], particularly regarding required OT counts, leftover hash lemma parameterization, and challenge derivation in the hybrid model.

2. We propose three robust variants of the protocol.
Each variant requires a different number of OTs, offering flexible trade-offs regarding round complexity and when the Receiver’s random input is determined.

3. We streamline the two-party signing protocol, introducing a non-reusable offline phase that maintains a compact state and enables an exceptionally fast online signing phase.

4. We provide rigorous, complete security proofs for all proposed protocols.
Although most of the proofs build on those of [DKLs24], they differ in several important respects.
For example, whereas [DKLs24] presents the protocol directly in its reduced-round form, we first analyze a variant with more rounds and then apply a round-compression argument to reduce the round complexity.
This approach isolates the precise properties needed at each step and improves readability.
In addition, the proof of the VOLE construction in [DKLs24] invokes the leftover-hash lemma three times, whereas our strategy requires only a single application.

### 3.1 ECDSA Signatures

For completeness, we provide the definition of ECDSA (see, e.g., [DKLs24]).

Construction 3.1: ECDSA signature

It assumes some group $\mathbb{G}$ for which the discrete logarithm problem is believed to be hard.
Let $G$ be the generator of $\mathbb{G},$ and $\mathsf{Scalar}$ be its associated scalar set $(\mathbb{Z}_{q}).$ Let $H$ be a hash function.

- $\mathsf{Gen}(1^\kappa)$: Choose a uniform $x\leftarrow \mathsf{Scalar}$ and set $X=x\cdot G.$ The public key $\mathsf{pk}=X$ and the secret key $\mathsf{sk}=x.$

- $\mathsf{Sign}_{\mathsf{sk}}(m)$: On input the private key $\mathsf{sk}$ and a message $m,$ choose a random $k\leftarrow \mathsf{Scalar}$ and let $R=k\cdot G.$ Let $r^{\mathsf{x}}$ be the $x$-coordinate of $R.$ Compute $s:=[k^{-1}\cdot (H(m)+\mathsf{sk}\cdot r^{\mathsf{x}})].$ (If $r^{\mathsf{x}}=0$ or $s=0$ then restart with a fresh choice of $k).$ Output the signature $\sigma =(r^{\mathsf{x}},s).$

- $\mathsf{Vrfy}_{\mathsf{pk}}(m,\sigma)$: Compute $R=(H(m)\cdot s^{-1})\cdot G+(r^{\mathsf{x}}\cdot s^{-1})\mathsf{pk}.$ Verify that the $x$-coordinate of $R$ is $r^{\mathsf{x}},$ and if so, output 1.

### 3.2 Building Blocks

The random oracle model.
For simplicity, we assume that all the protocols are in the random oracle model.
The functionality for a random oracle is defined as follows:

Functionality 3.2: The Random Oracle Functionality $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{ROM}}^{\mathcal{D}}$

The functionality is parametrized by a domain $\mathcal{D}.$

• Upon receiving $(\mathsf{ROM},x),$ the functionality checks if a pair $(x,y)$ is stored for some $y\in \mathcal{D}.$ If yes, it returns $y.$ Else, it chooses a random $y\in \mathcal{D},$ stores $(x,y),$ and returns $y.$ We sometimes use $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{ROM}}^{\mathsf{size}}$ as $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{ROM}}^{\{0,1\}^{\mathsf{size}}}.$

Commitments.
Commitments can easily be instantiated using a random oracle, and implement the following functionality (see, e.g., [Lin24]):

Functionality 3.3: The commitment functionality $F_{\mathsf{com}}$

The functionality $F_{\mathsf{com}}$ works with parties $P_{0},P_{1}$ and the adversary $\mathcal{S},$ as follows:

- Upon receiving $(\mathsf{commit},\mathsf{sid},i,x)$ from party $P_{i};$ store $(\mathsf{sid},i,x)$ and send $(\mathsf{receipt},\mathsf{sid},i)$ to $\mathcal{S}$ and the honest party.

- Upon receiving $(\mathsf{decommit},\mathsf{sid},i)$ from the party $P_{i},$ if $(\mathsf{sid},i,x)$ is recorded, then send $(\mathsf{decommit},\mathsf{sid},i,x)$ to $\mathcal{S}$ and to the honest party.

Sender Random OT. We prove that the protocol is secure in the Sender Random OT [ALSZ17]. In this formalization, the honest Sender also gets random inputs from the functionality.
However, malicious Sender cannot freely choose its inputs, and those are determined by the functionality as well.

Functionality $3.4:\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{m}\times \mathsf{RSOT}_{\mathcal{D}}}-$ Sender Random OT

The functionality interacts with the Sender and the Receiver, and with an ideal adversary $\mathcal{S}$ who can instruct to abort at any time.
The domain is $\mathcal{D}.$

1. If the receiver is corrupt, then receive $(\mathsf{receiverInput},\mathsf{sid},\mathbf{w},(\mathbf{v}_{j}^{w_{j}})_{j\in [\mathsf{m}]})$ from the Receiver with $\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},...,w_{\mathsf{m}})\in \{0,1\}^{\mathsf{m}}$ and $\mathbf{v}_{j}^{w_{j}}\in \mathcal{D}$ for every $j\in [\mathsf{m}].$ Sample $\mathbf{v}_{j}^{1-w_{j}}\in \mathcal{D}$ for every $j\in [\mathsf{m}].$

2. If the sender is corrupt, or if both parties are honest, then receive $(\mathsf{receiverInput},\mathsf{sid},\mathbf{w})$ from the receiver.
Sample $\mathbf{v}_{j}^{0},\mathbf{v}_{j}^{1}\in \mathcal{D}$ for every $j\in [\mathsf{m}].$

Send $(\mathsf{senderOutput},\mathsf{sid},(\mathbf{v}_{j}^{0},\mathbf{v}_{j}^{1})_{j\in [\mathsf{m}]})$ to the Sender and $(\mathsf{receiverOutput},\mathsf{sid},\mathbf{w},(\mathbf{v}_{j}^{w_{j}})_{j\in [\mathsf{m}]})$ to the Receiver.

## 4 Random Vector Oblivious Linear Evaluation

In this section, we provide a full description and proof for the oblivious linear evaluation sub-protocol.
We first provide the functionality in Section 4.1.

<a id="section-4-1-functionality"></a>

### 4.1 Functionality

Looking ahead, our functionality is of a random VOLE where the inputs of both the Sender and Receiver are chosen uniformly (as opposed to [DKLs24], in which the inputs of the Sender are not random). Moreover, as no intermediate outputs are needed in our two-party signing, the formulation of the functionality is slightly simpler compared to [DKLs24].

Functionality $4.1:\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{RVOLE}}:$ Random VOLE with delayed receiver’s input

The functionality interacts with two parties, a Sender and a Receiver, and the ideal adversary $\mathcal{S}$ who can instruct the functionality to abort a session at any time.
For concreteness, $\ell $ is the number of multiplications, and the multiplications are performed over $\mathsf{Scalar}.$ Multiplication:

- If the Sender is honest and sends $(\mathsf{multiply},\mathsf{sid})$ to the functionality, and if the Receiver is corrupted, $\mathsf{sid}$ is fresh, then notify the ideal adversary.
Receive back $(\mathsf{receiverInput},\mathsf{sid},\beta ,\mathbf{r})$ for $\beta \in \mathsf{Scalar},\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},...,r_{\ell })\in \mathsf{Scalar}^{\ell }.$ Sample $\alpha =(\alpha _{1},...,\alpha _{\ell })\in \mathsf{Scalar}^{\ell },$ and compute for every $i\in [\ell ]:t_{i}=\alpha _{i}\cdot \beta -r_{i}.$

Send $(\mathsf{senderOutput},\mathsf{sid},\alpha ,\mathbf{t})$ to the Sender for $\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},...,t_{\ell }).$

- If the Receiver is honest and sends $(\mathsf{multiply},\mathsf{sid})$ to the functionality, and the Sender is corrupted, $\mathsf{sid}$ is fresh, then notify the ideal adversary.
Receive back $(\mathsf{senderInput},\mathsf{sid},\alpha ,\mathbf{t})$ for $\alpha =(\alpha _{1},...,\alpha _{\ell }),\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},...,t_{\ell })$ both in $\mathsf{Scalar}^{\ell }.$ Sample $\beta \in \mathsf{Scalar}$ and set for every $i\in [\ell ],$ compute $r_{i}=\alpha _{i}\cdot \beta -t_{i}.$ Send $(\mathsf{receiverOutput},\mathsf{sid},\beta ,\mathbf{r})$ to the Receiver.

- If both parties are honest and send $(\mathsf{multiply},\mathsf{sid})$ to the functionality, then sample $\alpha \in \mathsf{Scalar}^{\ell },$ $\beta \in \mathsf{Scalar}.$ Sample $\mathbf{t}=(t_{1},...,t_{\ell })\in \mathsf{Scalar}^{\ell },$ and set for every $i\in [\ell ]$ the value $r_{i}=\alpha _{i}\cdot \beta -t_{i}.$ Send $(\mathsf{senderOutput},\mathsf{sid},\alpha ,\mathbf{t})$ to the Sender and $(\mathsf{receiverOutput},\mathsf{sid},\beta ,\mathbf{r})$ to the Receiver.

<a id="section-4-2-the-protocol"></a>

### 4.2 The Protocol

We provide three different variants for the protocol, where they are numbered with an increasing number of OTs needed.
Nevertheless, as we focus on the two-party setting, and advocate using Variant II, we describe it first to ease translation into an implementation.
We recall all three variants:

1. Variant I (Section 4.3): Using additional rounds, and picking $\mathbf{g}$ at the end.
This reduces the overall number of OTs, as $\mathbf{g}$ is chosen uniformly at random at the end of the protocol.

2. Variant II (Section 4.2.1): This is essentially Variant I, after applying a round compressing transformation, and $\mathbf{g}$ is chosen by applying the random oracle on the transcript of the protocol.

3. Variant III (Section 4.4 $):\mathbf{g}$ is chosen at the onset of the computation, and therefore requires the most number of OTs.
This is the original protocol by [DKLs24].

We also remark that a “tighter” functionality can be described for each variant.
For example, in Variant III, the Receiver’s input $\beta$ is known already at the end of the first round, and therefore one can describe a functionality which gives first $\beta$ to the Receiver, and later its additive share.
However, for simplicity, we provide a single functionality, which is a common ground for all three variants.

<a id="section-4-2-1-variant-ii-full-description"></a>

#### 4.2.1 Variant II: Full Description

We describe the protocol of [DKLs24] while deferring the choice of the “gadget vector” $(\mathbf{g})$ to the end of the protocol instead of the beginning.
We write the protocol in an “implementation-friendly” description, clearly identifying the input-output of each next-message function.

Protocol 4.2: VOLE Protocol in the $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{ROM}}-\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{RSOT}}$-hybrid model

#### The protocol:

1. Receiver Round 1

$\mathsf{VOLE}.\mathsf{R}.Round1(\mathsf{sid})$

Upon receiving $(\mathsf{multiply},\mathsf{sid})$ from the environment:

(a) The Receiver chooses a random $\mathbf{w}\leftarrow \{0,1\}^{\mathsf{m}},$ and calls $(\mathsf{receiverInput},\mathsf{sid}\Vert \mathtt{RSOT},\mathbf{w})$ to $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{m}\times \mathsf{RSOT}_{\mathsf{Scalar}^{\ell +1}}}$ (Functionality 3.4).

2. Sender Round 2: $((\alpha _{1},...,\alpha _{\ell }),(t_{1},...,t_{\ell }))\leftarrow \mathsf{VOLE}.\mathsf{S}.Round2(\mathsf{sid},\mathbf{T}^{0},\mathbf{T}^{1})$ Upon receiving $(\mathsf{multiply},\mathsf{sid})$ from the environment,

and upon receiving $(\mathsf{senderOutput},\mathsf{sid}\Vert \mathtt{RSOT},\mathbf{T}^{0},\mathbf{T}^{1})$ from $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{m}\times \mathsf{RSOT}_{\mathsf{Scalar}^{\ell +1}}}$ (Functionality 3.4):

(a) The Sender samples $\alpha _{1},...,\alpha _{\ell },\hat{\alpha },$ each in $\mathsf{Scalar},$ and sets

$$
\mathbf{\alpha}= ( \alpha_{1}, \dots , \alpha_{\ell}, \hat{\alpha}) \in \mathsf{Scalar}^{\ell + 1},
$$

and

$$
\mathbf A=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol\alpha\\\vdots\\\boldsymbol\alpha\end{pmatrix}\in\mathsf{Scalar}^{m\times(\ell+1)}
$$

i.e., $\mathbf{A}$ is a $\mathsf{m}\times (\ell +1)$ matrix, where all the rows are the same — $\boldsymbol\alpha.$

(b) Define:

$$
\mathbf{Q}= \mathbf{T}^{0}- \mathbf{T}^{1}+ \mathbf{A}\in \mathsf{Scalar}^{\mathsf{m}\times ( \ell + 1 )},
$$

and denote:

$$
\mathbf T=\mathbf T^0=\begin{pmatrix}
t_{1,1}&\ldots&t_{1,\ell}&\hat t_1\\
t_{2,1}&\ldots&t_{2,\ell}&\hat t_2\\
\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\
t_{m,1}&\ldots&t_{m,\ell}&\hat t_m
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

(c) The Sender computes $(\chi_1,\ldots,\chi_\ell)\leftarrow\mathcal F_{\mathrm{ROM}}^{\mathsf{Scalar}^{\ell}}(\mathsf{sid},\mathsf{chi},\mathbf Q)$.<sup>2</sup>

(d) We write $\boldsymbol\chi=(\chi_1,\ldots,\chi_\ell,1)$.

<sup>2</sup>In the plain model (after instantiating $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{RSOT}})-$ the input to the $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{ROM}}$ should also include the transcript of the oblivious transfer.

(e) The Sender computes:

$$
\mathbf{u}= \mathbf{T}\cdot \mathbf{\chi}= \left( \begin{array}{c c c c c}{\chi_{1}\cdot t_{1 , 1}}&{+ \ldots +}&{\chi_{\ell}\cdot t_{1 , \ell}}&{+}&{\hat{t}_{1}}\\{\ldots}&{}&{\ldots}&{}&{\ldots}\\{\chi_{1}\cdot t_{\mathsf{m}, 1}}&{+ \ldots +}&{\chi_{\ell}\cdot t_{\mathsf{m}, \ell}}&{+}&{\hat{t}_{\mathsf{m}}}\\ \end{array}\right) ,
$$

and

$v=\langle \alpha ,\chi \rangle =\chi _{1}\cdot \alpha _{1}+...+\chi _{\ell }\cdot \alpha _{\ell }+\hat{\alpha }.$

(f) Compute $H_{u}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{ROM}}^{2\kappa}(\mathsf{sid},\mathtt{u},\mathbf{u}).$

(g) The Sender defines $m_{2}=(\mathbf{Q},H_{u},v).$

(h) The Sender defines $\mathbf{g}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{ROM}}^{\mathsf{Scalar}^{\mathsf{m}}}(\mathsf{sid},\mathtt{gadget},m_{2}).$ See Footnote 2.

(i) Outgoing Message: The sender sends to the Receiver the message $m_{2}=(\mathbf{Q},H_{u},v).$

(j) Computing Output: The Sender outputs $(\alpha _{1},...,\alpha _{\ell }),(t_{1},...,t_{\ell }),$ where each $t_{i}$ is defined by:

$$
t_{i}= - \sum_{j = 1}^{\mathsf{m}}g_{j}\cdot t_{j , i}.
$$

3. Receiver Computes Output: $((r_{1},...,r_{\ell }),b)\leftarrow \mathsf{VOLE}.\mathsf{R}.\mathsf{ComputeOutput}(m_{2},\mathbf{S})$ Upon receiving $m_{2}=(\mathbf{Q},H_{u},v)$ from the Sender, and $(\mathsf{receiverOutput},\mathsf{sid}\Vert \mathtt{RSOT},\mathbf{S})$ from $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{m}\times \mathsf{RSOT}_{\mathsf{Scalar}^{\ell +1}}}:$

(a) Compute a matrix $\mathbf{R}\in \mathsf{Scalar}^{\mathsf{m}\times (\ell +1)}$ as follows.
For every $j\in [\mathsf{m}],i\in [\ell +1]:$

$$
\mathbf{R}_{j , i}= \mathbf{S}_{j , i}+ w_{j}\cdot \mathbf{Q}_{j , i},
$$

and write:

$$
\mathbf R=\begin{pmatrix}r_{1,1}&\cdots&r_{1,\ell}&\hat r_1\\r_{2,1}&\cdots&r_{2,\ell}&\hat r_2\\\vdots&&\vdots&\vdots\\r_{m,1}&\cdots&r_{m,\ell}&\hat r_m\end{pmatrix}
$$

Note that for every $j\in [\mathsf{m}],i\in [\ell ],$ it is supposed to hold that:

$$
r_{j , i}- t_{j , i}= w_{j}\cdot \alpha_{i}, \quad \hat{r}_{j}- \hat{t}_{j}= w_{j}\cdot \hat{\alpha}. \quad ( 1 )
$$

(b) Invoke $(\chi _{1},...,\chi _{\ell })\leftarrow \mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{ROM}}^{\mathsf{Scalar}^{\ell }}(\mathsf{sid},\mathtt{chi},\mathbf{Q}).$ See Footnote 2.

(c) Let $\chi =(\chi _{1},...,\chi _{\ell },1).$

(d) For every $j\in [\mathsf{m}],$ compute:

$$
u'_j=\langle\mathbf R_j,\boldsymbol\chi\rangle-w_j\cdot v
$$

and let $\mathbf u'=(u'_1,\ldots,u'_m)$. Then, verify that

$$
H_{u}= \mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{ROM}}^{2 \kappa}( \mathsf{sid}, \mathtt{u}, \mathbf{u}^{\prime}) .
$$

If not, then it aborts.

(e) The Receiver defines $\mathbf{g}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{ROM}}^{\mathsf{Scalar}^{\mathsf{m}}}(\mathsf{sid},\mathtt{gadget},m_{2}).$ See Footnote 2.

(f) The Receiver computes $b=\langle \mathbf{g},\mathbf{w}\rangle .$

(g) Output: The Receiver outputs $b$ and $(r_{1},...,r_{\ell }),$ each in $\mathsf{Scalar},$ such that:

$$
r_{i}= \sum_{j = 1}^{\mathsf{m}}g_{j}\cdot r_{j , i}.
$$

### 4.3 Variant I: Simpler Protocol – More Rounds

Protocol 4.2 uses “Fiat-Shamir-like” transformation in two steps to compress the number of rounds.
To simplify the analysis, we first analyze the protocol using additional rounds; we then apply the “Fiat-Shamir-like” transformation to obtain the real protocol, while reducing its security to the security of our additional-rounds protocol.
Concretely, the full protocol is as follows:

1. Round 1 – Receiver: The Receiver runs Step 1 in its entirety; in particular, it sends $(\mathsf{receiverInput},\mathsf{sid}\Vert \mathtt{RSOT},\mathbf{w})$ to $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{m}\times \mathsf{RSOT}_{\mathsf{Scalar}^{\ell +1}}}.$ 2. Round 2 – Sender: The Sender runs Step 2 until Step 2c, and it sends the message $\mathbf{Q}$ to Receiver.

3. Round 3 – Receiver: Instead of deriving $\chi$ from the random oracle as in the real protocol as in Step 2c, the receiver chooses it.
That is, the receiver chooses $(\chi _{1},...,\chi _{\ell })$ uniformly at random in $\mathsf{Scalar}^{\ell },$ and sends it to the Sender.

4. Round 4 – Sender: The Sender continues to run Step 2, from Step 2e to Step 2g.
It then sends $(\mathbf{u},v)$ to the Receiver.
For simplicity, we send $\mathbf{u}$ in its entirety instead of $H_{u}=$ $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{ROM}}^{2\kappa}(\mathsf{sid},\mathtt{u},\mathbf{u}).$

5. Round 5 – Receiver: Instead of deriving $\mathbf{g}$ from the random oracle as in Step 2h, the receiver samples $\mathbf{g}$ uniformly at random in $\mathsf{Scalar}^{\mathsf{m}}$ and sends it to the Sender after the verification.
Moreover, instead of querying the random oracle on $\mathbf{u}^{\prime},$ it verifies directly that $\mathbf{u}^{\prime}=\mathbf{u}$ where $\mathbf{u}$ was received from the Sender.
If the verification passes, it computes its output $b=\langle \mathbf{g},\mathbf{w}\rangle$ and $(r_{1},...,r_{\ell })$ as in Step 3.

6. The Sender computes its output: Similarly to Step 2j – given $\mathbf{g}$ the Sender can now compute its output $(t_{1},...,t_{\ell }).$

<a id="section-4-4-variant-iii-the-protocol-of-dkls24"></a>

### 4.4 Variant III: The Protocol of [DKLs24]

Protocol 4.2 is a small modification of the protocol described in [DKLs24], where the modifications are:

1. Sender Random OT instead of Endemic OT: We work in a different hybrid model than that of [DKLs24], which also implies different properties from the underlying OT. We review the importance of this modification in Section 4.5.

2. Defer the choice of the gadget vector: The gadget vector $\mathbf{g}$ in [DKLs24] is chosen at the onset of the computation, as a function of the $\mathsf{sid}$ only.
As such, the Receiver can obtain its value $b=\langle \mathbf{g},\mathbf{w}\rangle$ at the end of the first round (and also output it as an intermediate output). In contrast, we defer the choice of $\mathbf{g}$ to the end of the protocol.
This makes $b$ available to the Receiver only at the end of round 2, but reduces the number of OTs, as we will see in the analysis.

3. Number of masking elements: The construction [DKLs24] adds $\rho$ elements to each row $(i.e.,\mathbf{A}\in \mathsf{Scalar}^{\mathsf{m}\times (\ell +\rho )}),$ where those elements mask the information revealed when multiplying the rows with $\chi .$ We describe the protocol with just $\rho =1,$ which suffices for Secp256k1, and for most applications.
In fact, the description in [DKLs24] requires that $\rho =\lceil$ log $q/\kappa \rceil$ with $\kappa$ as the computational security parameter (and recall that log $q$ is the number of bits for representing an element in the domain of the multiplication). For Secp256k1 (log $q=256)$ and $\kappa =128,$ this implies that $\rho =\lceil 256/128\rceil =2$ elements have to be added.
This looks like a typo, and $\rho =\lceil \kappa /$ log $q\rceil =1$ suffices.
As we will see in the analysis, $\rho =1$ suffices as long as log $q\geq \kappa +2\cdot$ log $\mathsf{m},$ see Claim B.8. We presented the protocol when $\rho$ is fixed to one, and we just add this requirement to the theorem statement.

4. Fully-random VOLE: In the protocol of [DKLs24], the functionality is slightly different: The Sender inputs particular inputs $(a_{1},...,a_{\ell }),$ the random “input” $b$ of the Receiver is chosen by the protocol, and the parties compute additive shares of $(a_{1}\cdot b,...,a_{\ell }\cdot b).$ This is done by letting the Sender send $a_{i}-\alpha _{i}$ in the last round, and then the Receiver adds to each of its output shares the value $(a_{i}-\alpha _{i})\cdot b.$ We use fully random VOLE, and delay sending this message to the signing phase.
This adds a round to the final protocol, but enables the separation between a truly input-independent preprocessing phase and an extremely fast online phase.

<a id="section-4-5-attack-on-the-protocol-in-the-endemic-ot-hybrid-model"></a>

### 4.5 Attack on the Protocol in the Endemic OT-Hybrid Model

The VOLE protocol of [DKLs24] is proven in the Endemic OT [MR19]-hybrid model, which allows a malicious Sender to pick its inputs to the functionality.
We show that the VOLE is insecure in this hybrid model.
We first describe the functionality:

Functionality $4.3:\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{m}\times \mathsf{Endemic}_{\mathcal{D}}}-$ Endemic OT

The functionality interacts with the Sender and the Receiver, and with an ideal adversary $\mathcal{S}$ who can instruct to abort at any time.
The domain is $\mathcal{D}.$

1. If the Sender is corrupted, upon receiving $(\mathsf{senderInput},\mathsf{sid},\mathbf{T}^{0},\mathbf{T}^{1})$ for a fresh $\mathsf{sid}$ and $\mathbf{T}^{0},\mathbf{T}^{1}\in \mathcal{D}^{\mathsf{m}},$ then sample $\mathbf{w}\in \{0,1\}^{\mathsf{m}}.$

2. If the Receiver is corrupted, then upon receiving $(\mathsf{receiverInput},\mathsf{sid},\mathbf{w})$ from the adversary, sample $\mathbf{T}^{0},\mathbf{T}^{1}\in \mathcal{D}^{\mathsf{m}}.$

3. If both parties are honest, then upon receiving both $(\mathsf{senderInput},\mathsf{sid})$ and $(\mathsf{receiverInput},\mathsf{sid})$ for the same fresh $\mathsf{sid},$ then sample $\mathbf{w}\in \{0,1\}^{\mathsf{m}}$ and $\mathbf{T}^{0},\mathbf{T}^{1}\in \mathcal{D}^{\mathsf{m}}.$

For every $i\in [\mathsf{m}]$ set $\mathbf{S}_{i}=(1-w_{i})\cdot \mathbf{T}_{i}^{0}+w_{i}\cdot \mathbf{T}_{i}^{1}=\mathbf{T}_{i}^{0}+w_{i}\cdot (\mathbf{T}_{i}^{1}-\mathbf{T}_{i}^{0}).$ Send $(\mathsf{senderOutput},\mathsf{sid},\mathbf{T}^{0},\mathbf{T}^{1})$ to the Sender, and $(\mathsf{receiverOutput},\mathsf{sid},\mathbf{w},\mathbf{S})$ to the Receiver.

The following refers to variants II and III of the protocol, when working with the endemic OT-hybrid model.
The purpose of the protocol’s consistency check is to ensure that the Sender indeed places the same row everywhere in the matrix $\mathbf{A}.$ To verify this, a challenge vector $\chi =$ $(\chi _{1},...,\chi _{\ell },1)$ is sampled, and the Sender computes $\mathbf{u}=\mathbf{T}^{0}\cdot \chi$ and $v=\langle \mathbf{A}_{1},\chi \rangle .$ For security, it is crucial that whenever two rows differ, $\mathbf{A}_{i}\not =\mathbf{A}_{j},$ we have $\langle \mathbf{A}_{i},\chi \rangle \not =\langle \mathbf{A}_{j},\chi \rangle .$ In other words, $\chi$ acts as a universal hash function: for every $\mathbf{A}_{i}\not =\mathbf{A}_{j},$ the probability over random $\chi$ that $\langle \mathbf{A}_{i},\chi \rangle =\langle \mathbf{A}_{j},\chi \rangle$ is negligible.
See Claim B.7 in the analysis.

In [DKLs24], the challenge $\chi$ is derived via the random oracle by hashing $\mathbf{Q}$ (together with $\mathsf{sid},$ domain separators, etc.), but without incorporating anything that binds the OT phase (see Step 2c in the description of Protocol 4.2). In the OT-hybrid model, no transcript binds the OT phase, since the parties merely send inputs and receive outputs from the functionality.
Moreover, they cannot hash their OT inputs or outputs, since the two parties do not observe the same values.
In the endemic OT-hybrid model, where a corrupted sender may freely choose its OT inputs, this opens the door for the following attack, which completely nullifies the verification step:

- The corrupted Sender first samples $\mathbf{Q}\in \mathsf{Scalar}^{\mathsf{m}\times (\ell +1)}$ uniformly at random.

- It derives $\chi$ as a function of $\mathbf{Q}$ using the random oracle; concretely, it computes $(\chi _{1},...,\chi _{\ell })=$ $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{ROM}}^{\mathsf{Scalar}^{\ell }}(\mathsf{sid},\mathtt{chi},\mathbf{Q})$ and sets $\chi =(\chi _{1},...,\chi _{\ell },1).$

- It then chooses any matrix $\mathbf{A}$ it wishes, with arbitrarily inconsistent rows, leaving only the last column undetermined.

- It samples $v\leftarrow \mathsf{Scalar}$ uniformly at random.

- For every $j\in [\mathsf{m}],$ it sets the last coordinate of row $j$ of $\mathbf{A}$ to

$$
\hat{\alpha}_{j}= v - \chi_{1}\cdot \alpha_{j , 1}- \ldots - \chi_{\ell}\cdot \alpha_{j , \ell}\; .
$$

Note that for every $i,j\in[m]$ it holds that

$$
\langle\mathbf A_j,\boldsymbol\chi\rangle=v=\langle\mathbf A_i,\boldsymbol\chi\rangle,
$$

so the universal-hash test is perfectly neutralized.

- Since this is the endemic OT hybrid model, the Sender can pick its own inputs to the OT. The adversary samples $\mathbf{T}^{0}$ uniformly and sets $\mathbf{T}^{1}=\mathbf{T}^{0}+\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{Q}.$

- In the endemic OT phase, it sends $(\mathsf{senderInput},\mathsf{sid},\mathbf{T}^{0},\mathbf{T}^{1})$ to the endemic-OT functionality.
It computes $\mathbf{u}=\langle \mathbf{T}^{0},\chi \rangle$ honestly and sets $H_{u}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{ROM}}^{2\kappa}(\mathsf{sid},\mathtt{u},\mathbf{u}).$ It then sends $(\mathbf{Q},H_{u},v)$ to the honest Receiver.

- The honest Receiver recovers $\mathbf{R},$ derives the same $\chi ,$ and for every $j$ computes

$$
\begin{array}{r c l}{u_{j}^{\prime}}&{=}&{\langle \mathbf{R}_{j}, \mathbf{\chi}\rangle - w_{j}\cdot v}\\{}&{=}&{\langle \mathbf{S}_{j}+ w_{j}\cdot \mathbf{Q}_{j}, \mathbf{\chi}\rangle - w_{j}\cdot v}\\{}&{=}&{\langle \mathbf{T}_{j}^{0}+ w_{j}\cdot \mathbf{A}_{j}, \mathbf{\chi}\rangle - w_{j}\cdot v}\\{}&{=}&{\langle \mathbf{T}_{j}^{0}, \mathbf{\chi}\rangle + w_{j}\langle \mathbf{A}_{j}, \mathbf{\chi}\rangle - w_{j}\cdot v}\\{}&{=}&{u_{j}+ w_{j}v - w_{j}\cdot v}\\{}&{=}&{u_{j},}\\ \end{array}
$$

so the verification step always passes, although the rows are not the same.

- This breaks the correctness property of the protocol.

How to prevent this attack.
We describe two complementary countermeasures.

Include the OT transcript when deriving $\chi .$ When applying a Fiat-Shamir-like transformation to a simpler interactive protocol, it is standard to hash the entire transcript up to that point.
In the OT-hybrid model, there is no such transcript, but in a real protocol the OT execution does generate one.
This is helpful if the transcript of the OT protocol commits the sender to its input, and the concrete OT protocol that is used has to satisfy that property.
Incorporating this transcript into the hash that produces $\chi$ forces $\chi$ to depend on the OT inputs.

Use Sender Random OT. We prove that the protocol is secure when instantiated with the Sender Random OT functionality (see Functionality 3.4). This functionality prevents a corrupted sender from choosing its OT inputs.
In Sender Random OT, the matrices $\mathbf{T}^{0}$ and $\mathbf{T}^{1}$ are generated by the functionality, and the sender has no control over them.
Since $\mathbf{Q}$ and $\mathbf{A}$ (given $\mathbf{T}^{0},\mathbf{T}^{1})$ are in one-to-one correspondence, any choice of $\mathbf{A}$ uniquely determines $\mathbf{Q},$ which in turn uniquely determines $\chi .$ We show that it is infeasible to find a “bad” pair $(\mathbf{Q},\chi )$ enabling the attack above; with overwhelming probability, if $\mathbf{A}_{i}\not =\mathbf{A}_{j}$ then $\langle \mathbf{A}_{i},\chi \rangle \not =\langle \mathbf{A}_{j},\chi \rangle .$ We show that the protocol is secure in this hybrid model, while applying the random oracle only on $\mathbf{Q}$ and not on the OT-transcript.
Nevertheless, in implementations we recommend also including the OT-transcript.

## 5 Applying Leftover Hash Lemma: Various Games and their Required Parameters

The three variants in Section 4 refer to three different security games that use the leftover-hash-lemma [ILL89], which results in different number of OTs, $\mathsf{m}.$ In this section, we formalize the three different security games.
First, recall that the leftover hash lemma compares the uniform distribution to a random choice of a hash function from a universal hash, followed by an output of that hash on a random input.
We start with the definition of a universal hash.

Definition 5.1. Let $\mathcal{H}$ be a family of hash functions from $\mathcal{X}\rightarrow \mathcal{Y}.\mathcal{H}$ is a universal family if for every $x_{1},x_{2}\in \mathcal{X}:$ Pr $[h(x_{1})=h(x_{2})]\leq 1/|\mathcal{Y}|,$ where $h$ is chosen uniformly from $\mathcal{H}.$

Recall that the min-entropy of a random variable $X$ is $-$ log(max $x\,p(x))$.

<a id="section-5-1-variant-i-plain-leftover-hash-lemma"></a>

### 5.1 Variant I: Plain Leftover Hash Lemma

Lemma 5.2 ([ILL89]). Let $\mathcal{H}$ be a universal hash function family.
Let $X$ be any probability distribution over $\mathcal{X}$ that has min entropy $\delta .$ Let $H$ be the uniform distribution over $\mathcal{H},$ and let $U_{\mathcal{Y}}$ be the uniform distribution over $\mathcal{Y}.$ Then:

$$
\mathsf{SD}\left( ( H , H ( X ) ) , ( H , U_{\mathcal{Y}}) \right) \leq \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{| \mathcal{Y}|}{2^{\delta}}}.
$$

Henceforth, we denote

$$
\epsilon_{\mathsf{LHL}}: = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{| \mathcal{Y}| / 2^{\delta}}\quad ( 2 )
$$

Concrete instantiation in our setting.
We consider Variant I of the protocol where the Receiver chooses $\mathbf{g}$ at the end of the protocol, and sends it to the Sender.

The proof assumes that the corrupted Sender sends messages that may be malformed, i.e., it may cheat in some of them.
In the analysis, we can delay the choice of the Receiver’s input $\mathbf{w}$ until after the Sender’s messages are fixed.
For a given transcript sent by the Sender, some choices of $\mathbf{w}$ will cause the Receiver to detect cheating, while others will not.
Hence, the messages sent by the corrupted Sender implicitly induce a set of constraints on $\mathbf{w}:$ if the verification succeeds and the Sender is not caught cheating, then $\mathbf{w}$ must satisfy these constraints.
In the final step of the proof, we show that under these constraints, the value $b=\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{w})=\langle \mathbf{g},\mathbf{w}\rangle$ remains close to uniform.

For a set of constraints $\mathbf{c}=\mathsf{constraints}=\{(j,\rho )\in [\mathsf{m}]\times \{0,1\}\},$ let $W_{\mathbf{c}}$ be the uniform distribution over $\{0,1\}^{\mathsf{m}}$ that is consistent with $\mathbf{c},i.e.,$ for every $(j,\rho )\in \mathbf{c}$ we fix $w_{j}=\rho$ and set all the other indices uniformly at random.
Thus, $W_{\mathbf{c}}$ has a min-entropy of $\mathsf{m}-|\mathbf{c}|.$ We have the following:

- The corrupted Sender might send messages that implicitly fixes some set of constraints $\mathbf{c}=\{(j,\rho )\in [\mathsf{m}]\times \{0,1\}\},$ such that $|\mathbf{c}|\leq \mathsf{s}.$ For each such constraint, the Sender takes a risk of getting caught with probability $1/2,$ and therefore it suffices to bound the number of constraints by $\mathsf{s}.$

- Let $\mathcal{W}_{\mathbf{c}}$ to be the set of all binary strings of length $\mathsf{m}$ that are consistent with $\mathbf{c}.$ That is, for every $(j,\rho )$ and for every $\mathbf{w}\in \mathcal{W}_{\mathbf{c}}$ it holds that $w_{j}=\rho .$

- Let $W_{\mathbf{c}}$ be the uniform distribution over $\mathcal{W}_{\mathbf{c}}.$ Thus, $W_{\mathbf{c}}$ has min entropy $\mathsf{m}-|\mathbf{c}|\geq \mathsf{m}-\mathsf{s}.$

- The set $\mathbf{g}=(g_{1},...,g_{\mathsf{m}})$ defines a hash function over $\mathsf{Scalar},$ where the input comes from $\mathcal{W}_{\mathbf{c}}:$ given $\mathbf{w}$ that is consistent with $\mathbf{c},$ the hash is defined as $\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{w})=\langle \mathbf{g},\mathbf{w}\rangle .$ We show that this is a universal hash function.
For every fixed set of constraints $\mathbf{c}$ with $|\mathbf{c}|\leq \mathsf{s},$ and for every $\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\in \mathcal{W}_{\mathbf{c}}$ with $\mathbf{w}\not =\mathbf{w}^{\prime },$ there must exist an index $j$ for which $w_{j}\not =w_{j}^{\prime }.$ Without loss of generality, assume that $w_{j}-w_{j}^{\prime}=1.$ Then:

$$
\operatorname*{Pr}\left[ \langle \mathbf{g}, \mathbf{w}\rangle = \langle \mathbf{g}, \mathbf{w}^{\prime}\rangle \right] = \operatorname*{Pr}\left[ g_{j}= - \sum_{k \neq j}( w_{k}^{\prime}- w_{k}) \cdot g_{k}\right] = \frac{1}{| \mathsf{Scalar}|}.
$$

Putting it all together, we get that

Claim 5.3. For every fixed set of constraints $\mathbf{c}\subseteq [\mathsf{m}]\times \{0,1\}$ of cardinality at most $\mathsf{s},$ it holds that:

$$
\mathsf{SD}\left( ( \mathbf{G}, \mathbf{G}( W_{\mathbf{c}}) ) , \quad ( \mathbf{G}, U_{\mathsf{Scalar}}) \right) \leq \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{| \mathsf{Scalar}|}{2^{\mathsf{m}- \mathsf{s}}}},
$$

This is bounded by $2^{-\mathsf{s}}$ when $\mathsf{m}\geq$ log $q+3\cdot \mathsf{s}.$

### 5.2 Variant II: Leftover Hash Lemma with Adversarially-Limitedly-Chosen Hash

We now explore Variant II: instead of choosing the hash $\mathbf{g}$ uniformly at random by the Receiver at the end of the protocol, $\mathbf{g}$ is chosen by the random oracle.
That is, $\mathbf{g}$ is a result of the random oracle applied on the message that the corrupted Sender is about to send.
This gives the adversary an additional capability: It can try different messages and see their corresponding $\mathbf{g}$ (by querying the random oracle). That is, it can try different messages before sending its actual message.

We model it as an adversary that can provide multiple sources, all of which have high-min-entropy, and for each source an independent hash is chosen.
Then, after seeing the chosen hash, the adversary can pick which source to proceed with.
Taking a worst-case adversary, we can assume it proceeds with the “weakest” hash, i.e., the hash for which $\mathsf{SD}(h_{i}(X_{i}),U_{\mathcal{Y}})$ is the largest.
We have the following two distributions:

$\mathsf{Dist}_{1,Q}$:

- Let $X_{1},...,X_{Q},$ be any probability distributions over $\mathcal{X}$ with min entropy $\delta$ each.

- Choose $h_{1},...,h_{Q}$ uniformly and independently at random from $\mathcal{H}.$

- Let $i=\mathsf{argmax}(\mathsf{SD}(h_{i}(X_{i}),U_{\mathcal{Y}})).$

- Output: $(h_{i},h_{i}(X_{i})).$

$\operatorname{Dist}_{2,Q}:$

- Let $X_{1},...,X_{Q}$ be any probability distributions over $\mathcal{X}$ with min entropy $\delta$ each.

- Choose $h_{1},...,h_{Q}$ uniformly and independently at random from $\mathcal{H}.$

- Let $i=\mathsf{argmax}(\mathsf{SD}(h_{i}(X_{i}),U_{\mathcal{Y}})).$

- Output: $(h_i,U_Y)$.

Lemma 5.4. For every $0<\varepsilon <1$ and $Q>1$ it holds that:

$$
\mathsf{SD}( \mathbf{Dist}_{1 , Q}, \mathbf{Dist}_{2 , Q}) \leq Q \cdot \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{| \mathcal{Y}| / 2^{\delta}}\cdot \epsilon^{- 1}+ \epsilon .
$$

Proof.
For a fixed probability distribution $X_{i}$ and a fixed hash $h,$ define:

$$
\Delta_{h , X_{i}}= \mathsf{SD}( h ( X_{i}) , U_{\mathcal{Y}}) .
$$

We now consider the expected value of $\Delta _{h_{i},X_{i}}$ for a random $h_{i}$ (and the probability distribution $X_{i}$ is fixed). We have:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_{h_i\leftarrow\mathcal H}(\Delta_{h_i,X_i})&=\sum_{h\in\mathcal H}\Pr[H=h]\cdot\Delta_{h,X_i}\\
&=\sum_{h\in\mathcal H}\Pr[H=h]\cdot\left(\frac12\sum_{y\in\mathcal Y}|\Pr[h(X_i)=y]-\Pr[U_{\mathcal Y}=y]|\right)\\
&=\frac12\sum_{h\in\mathcal H}\sum_{y\in\mathcal Y}|\Pr[(H,H(X_i))=(h,y)]-\Pr[(H,U_{\mathcal Y})=(h,y)]|\\
&=\mathsf{SD}\bigl((H,H(X_i)),(H,U_{\mathcal Y})\bigr)\leq\epsilon_{\mathsf{LHL}}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Using Markov’s inequality, for every $\varepsilon \geq 0$ it holds that:

$$
\operatorname*{Pr}_{h_{i}\leftarrow H}\left[ \Delta_{h_{i}, X_{i}}\geq \epsilon \right] \leq E ( \Delta_{H_{i}, X_{i}}) \cdot \epsilon^{- 1}\leq \epsilon_{\mathsf{LHL}}\cdot \epsilon^{- 1}.
$$

Let $\mathsf{Bad}$ be the event (either in $\mathsf{Dist}_{1,Q}$ or $\mathsf{Dist}_{2,Q})$ such that, for each $X_{i}$ (for $i\in [Q])$ we sample $h_{i}$ uniformly and independently at random and it holds that $\Delta _{h_{i},X_{i}}\geq \varepsilon .$ By a union bound over all $Q$ pairs $(h_{i},X_{i}),$ we get that:

$$
\operatorname*{Pr}\left[{\mathsf{Bad}}\right] = \operatorname*{Pr}\left[{\exists_{i \in [ Q ]}\quad \Delta_{h_{i}, X_{i}}\geq \epsilon}\right] \leq Q \cdot \epsilon_{\mathsf{LHL}}\cdot \epsilon^{- 1}.
$$

We have:

1. In both experiments the probability of $\mathsf{Bad}$ is bounded by $Q\cdot \varepsilon _{\mathsf{LHL}}\cdot \varepsilon ^{-1}.$

2. The statistical distance between the two experiments when $\mathsf{Bad}$ does not occur is at most $\varepsilon ,$ as it does not matter which $i$ is chosen.

We have that (and by applying Eq. (2) this completes the proof):

$$
\mathsf{SD}(\mathsf{Dist}_{1,Q},\mathsf{Dist}_{2,Q})
$$

$$
\begin{array}{l l}{=}&{\mathsf{SD}\left({\mathsf{Dist}_{1 , Q}\mid \mathsf{Bad}, \quad \mathsf{Dist}_{2 , Q}\mid \mathsf{Bad}}\right) \cdot \operatorname*{Pr}\left[{\mathsf{Bad}}\right]}\\{}&{+ \mathsf{SD}\left({\mathsf{Dist}_{1 , Q}\mid \overline{{\mathsf{Bad}}}, \quad \mathsf{Dist}_{2 , Q}\mid \overline{{\mathsf{Bad}}}}\right) \cdot \operatorname*{Pr}\left[{\overline{{\mathsf{Bad}}}}\right]}\\{\leq}&{Q \cdot \epsilon_{\mathsf{LHL}}\cdot \epsilon^{- 1}+ \epsilon .}\\ \end{array}
$$

Instantiation in Our Setting This variant corresponds to Variant II, and the exact description of Protocol 4.2. We have the following claim:

Claim 5.5. Let $\mathsf{m}\geq$ log $q+3\cdot \mathsf{s}+2\cdot \kappa .$ For the following two distributions, it holds that:

$$
\mathsf{SD}( \mathbf{Dist}_{1 , Q}, \mathbf{Dist}_{2 , Q}) \leq Q \cdot 2^{- \kappa}+ 2^{- \mathsf{s}},
$$

when the distributions are defined as:

$\operatorname{Dist}_{1,Q}:$

- Fix constraints $\mathbf{c}_{1},...,\mathbf{c}_{Q}$ and let $W_{\mathbf{c}_{1}},...,W_{\mathbf{c}_{Q}}$ be the corresponding uniform distributions conditioned on being consistent with the corresponding constraints.

- Choose $\mathbf{g}_{1},...,\mathbf{g}_{Q}$ uniformly and independently at random from $\mathsf{Scalar}^{\mathsf{m}}.$

- Let $i=\mathsf{argmax}(\mathsf{SD}(\mathbf{g}_{i}(W_{\mathbf{c}_{i}}),U_{\mathsf{Scalar}})).$

- Output: $(\mathbf{g}_{i},\mathbf{g}_{i}(W_{\mathbf{c}_{i}})).$

$\operatorname{Dist}_{2,Q}:$

- Fix constraints $\mathbf{c}_{1},...,\mathbf{c}_{Q}$ and let $W_{\mathbf{c}_{1}},...,W_{\mathbf{c}_{Q}}$ be the corresponding uniform distributions conditioned on being consistent with the corresponding constraints.

- Choose $\mathbf{g}_{1},...,\mathbf{g}_{Q}$ uniformly and independently at random from $\mathsf{Scalar}^{\mathsf{m}}.$

- Let $i=\mathsf{argmax}(\mathsf{SD}(\mathbf{g}_{i}(W_{\mathbf{c}_{i}}),U_{\mathsf{Scalar}})).$

- Output: $(\mathbf{g}_{i},U_{\mathsf{Scalar}}).$

Proof.
Using Lemma 5.4:

$$
\mathsf{SD}( \mathbf{Dist}_{1 , Q}, \quad \mathbf{Dist}_{2 , Q}) \leq Q \cdot \epsilon_{\mathsf{LHL}}\cdot \epsilon^{- 1}+ \epsilon .
$$

When $\varepsilon =2^{-\mathsf{s}},$ it suffices to require that $\varepsilon _{\mathsf{LHL}}\cdot \varepsilon ^{-1}\leq 2^{-\kappa},$ and so (recall Eq. (2)):

$$
\begin{array}{r l r}{\epsilon_{\mathsf{LHL}}\cdot \epsilon^{- 1}}&{\leq}&{2^{- \kappa},}\\{\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\frac{| \mathsf{Scalar}|}{2^{\mathsf{m}- \mathsf{s}}}}\cdot 2^{\mathsf{s}}}&{\leq}&{2^{- \kappa}.}\end{array}
$$

This implies that

$$
2^{\mathsf{m}- \mathsf{s}}\quad \geq \quad \frac{1}{4}\cdot | \mathsf{Scalar}| \cdot 2^{2 ( \mathsf{s}+ \kappa )}
$$

and so we require that $\mathsf{m}\geq$ log $q+3\cdot \mathsf{s}+2\cdot \kappa .$

### 5.3 Variant III: Leftover Hash Lemma with Adversarial Source-Fixing

This variant is the one needed in the original construction of [DKLs24] and corresponds to Variant III. In this variant, the hash is first chosen $h\leftarrow H.$ Then, the adversary can pick the source $X$ as a function of the hash function.
For worst-case, we might assume that it picks the “worst” source for that function, i.e., the one for which $\mathsf{SD}(h(X),U_{\mathcal{Y}})$ is maximal.

$\operatorname{Dist}_{1,\widetilde X}:$

- Choose $h$ uniformly at random from $\mathcal{H}.$

- Let $\tilde{X}$ be the set of all probability distributions over $\mathcal{X}$ with min-entropy $\delta .$ Let $X=\mathsf{argmax}_{X\in \tilde{X}}(\mathsf{SD}(h(X),U_{\mathcal{Y}})).$

$\operatorname{Dist}_{2,\widetilde X}:$

- Choose $h$ uniformly at random from $\mathcal{H}.$

- Sample $y$ uniformly in $\mathcal{Y},$ and output $(h,y).$

- Sample $x$ according to $X$ and output $(h,h(x)).$

Lemma 5.6. For every $0<\varepsilon <1$ it holds that:

$$
\mathsf{SD}\left({\mathsf{Dist}_{1 , \tilde{X}}, \mathsf{Dist}_{2 , \tilde{X}}}\right) \leq \left|{\tilde{X}}\right| \cdot \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{| \mathcal{Y}| / 2^{\delta}}\cdot \epsilon^{- 1}+ \epsilon .
$$

Proof.
Similarly to the proof of Lemma 5.4, we obtain that for a fixed source $X$ and random $h,$ for every $\varepsilon \geq 0$ it holds that:

$$
\operatorname*{Pr}_{h \leftarrow H}\left[ \Delta_{h , X}> \epsilon \right] \leq E ( \Delta_{h , X}) \cdot \epsilon^{- 1}\leq \epsilon_{\mathsf{LHL}}\cdot \epsilon^{- 1}.
$$

This implies that for a random $h\leftarrow H:$

$$
\operatorname*{Pr}_{h \leftarrow H}\left[ \exists X \in \tilde{X}{~}\operatorname{s . t . ~}{~}{\Delta}_{h , X}> \epsilon \right] \leq \sum_{X \in \tilde{X}}\operatorname*{Pr}_{h}\left[{\Delta}_{h , X}> \epsilon \right] \leq \left| \tilde{X}\right| \cdot \epsilon_{\mathsf{LHL}}\cdot \epsilon^{- 1}{~}.
$$

Let $\mathsf{Bad}$ be the event (either in $\mathsf{Dist}_{1,\tilde{X}}$ or $\mathsf{Dist}_{2,\tilde{X}})$ such that a bad $h$ is chosen, i.e., an $h$ for which there exists some $X\in \tilde{X}$ such that $\Delta _{h,X}>\varepsilon .$ We have:

1. In both experiments the probability of $\mathsf{Bad}$ is bounded by $|\widetilde{\mathcal X}|\cdot\epsilon_{\mathrm{LHL}}\cdot\epsilon^{-1}$.

2. The statistical distance between the two experiments conditioned on $\mathsf{Bad}$ does not occur is at most $\varepsilon ,$ as it does not matter which source is chosen.

We have that (and recall Eq. (2)):

$$
\begin{aligned}{}&{{}\mathsf{SD}\left( \mathsf{Dist}_{1 , \tilde{X}}, \mathsf{Dist}_{2 , \tilde{X}}\right)}\\{}&{{}\quad = \quad \mathsf{SD}\left( \mathsf{Dist}_{1 , \tilde{X}}\mid \mathsf{Bad}, \quad \mathsf{Dist}_{2 , \tilde{X}}\mid \mathsf{Bad}\right) \cdot \operatorname*{Pr}\left[ \mathsf{Bad}\right]}\\{}&{{}\quad + \mathsf{SD}\left( \mathsf{Dist}_{1 , \tilde{X}}\mid \overline{{\mathsf{Bad}}}, \quad \mathsf{Dist}_{2 , \tilde{X}}\mid \overline{{\mathsf{Bad}}}\right) \cdot \operatorname*{Pr}\left[ \overline{{\mathsf{Bad}}}\right]}\\{}&{{}\quad \leq \quad \left| \tilde{X}\right| \cdot \epsilon_{\mathsf{LHL}}\cdot \epsilon^{- 1}+ \epsilon .}\\ \end{aligned}
$$

#### 5.3.1 Instantiation in Our Setting

In variant III – we wish to bound the statistical distance by $2^{-\mathsf{s}}.$ This requires increasing $\mathsf{m}$ quite substantially.

Claim 5.7. Assume that $\mathsf{m}\geq$ log $q+7\mathsf{s}+2$ log($\mathsf{s}+1)+2\mathsf{s}\cdot$ log $\frac{e\cdot m}{s}$, and let $\mathcal{C}$ be the set of all possible constraints in $[\mathsf{m}]\times \{0,1\}$ of cardinality at most $\mathsf{s}.$ Then,

$$
{\mathsf{SD}}({\mathbf{Dist}}_{1 ,{\mathcal{C}}},{\mathbf{Dist}}_{2}) \leq 2^{-{\mathsf{s}}},
$$

where $\operatorname{Dist}_{1,\mathcal C},$ Dist<sup>2</sup> are defined as follows:

$\operatorname{Dist}_{1,\mathcal C}:$

- Choose $\mathbf{g}$ uniformly at random from $\mathsf{Scalar}^{\mathsf{m}}.$

- $\mathcal C$ is the set of all constraints of cardinality at most $\mathsf{s}.$

$\operatorname{Dist}_{2}:$

- Choose $\mathbf{g}$ uniformly at random from $\mathsf{Scalar}^{\mathsf{m}}.$

- Sample $y$ uniformly in $\mathsf{Scalar},$ and output $(\mathbf{g},y).$

- Let $\mathbf{c}=\mathsf{argmax}_{\mathbf{c}\in \mathcal{C}}(\mathsf{SD}(\mathbf{g}(W_{\mathbf{c}}),U_{\mathsf{Scalar}})).$

- Sample $\mathbf{w}$ according to $W_{\mathbf{c}}$ and output $(\mathbf{g},\langle \mathbf{g},\mathbf{w}\rangle ).$

Proof.
We use Lemma 5.6, with $\tilde{X}=\mathcal{C}.$ We set $\varepsilon =2^{-\mathsf{s}},$ and it suffices to show that

$$
|\mathcal C|\cdot\epsilon_{\mathsf{LHL}}\cdot2^s\leq2^{-s},
$$

i.e., we have to make sure that (recall Eq. (2):)

$$
| \mathcal{C}| \cdot \frac{1}{2}\cdot \sqrt{\frac{| \mathsf{Scalar}|}{2^{\mathsf{m}- \mathsf{s}}}}\cdot 2^{\mathsf{s}}\quad \leq \quad 2^{- \mathsf{s}},
$$

In our setting, $\tilde{X}$ is the set of all constraints $\mathbf{c}$ with cardinality at most $\mathsf{s}.$ We can bound:

$$
| \mathcal{C}| \leq \sum_{k = 0}^{\mathsf{s}}\binom{\mathsf{m}}{k}2^{k}\leq ( \mathsf{s}+ 1 ) \cdot \Big ( \frac{e \cdot \mathsf{m}}{\mathsf{s}}\Big )^{\mathsf{s}}\cdot 2^{\mathsf{s}},
$$

i.e., we might consider between $k\in [0,...,\mathsf{s}]$ constraints; each constraint involves choosing $k$ locations and fixing $k$ particular bits.
Thus, we request that:

$$
( \mathsf{s}+ 1 ) \cdot \left( \frac{e \cdot \mathsf{m}}{\mathsf{s}}\right)^{\mathsf{s}}\cdot 2^{\mathsf{s}}\cdot \frac{1}{2}\cdot \sqrt{\frac{| \mathsf{Scalar}|}{2^{\mathsf{m}- \mathsf{s}}}}\cdot 2^{\mathsf{s}}\leq 2^{- \mathsf{s}}
$$

Taking log of both sides:

$$
\operatorname{log}( \mathsf{s}+ 1 ) + \mathsf{s}\operatorname{log}( e \mathsf{m}/ \mathsf{s}) + \mathsf{s}- 1 + \frac{1}{2}( \operatorname{log}q - ( \mathsf{m}- \mathsf{s}) ) + \mathsf{s}\leq - \mathsf{s}
$$

Multiply through by 2:

$$
2 \operatorname{log}( \mathsf{s}+ 1 ) + 2 \mathsf{s}\operatorname{log}( e \mathsf{m}/ \mathsf{s}) + 4 \mathsf{s}- 2 + \operatorname{log}q - \mathsf{m}+ \mathsf{s}\leq - 2 \mathsf{s}
$$

Rearrange for $\mathsf{m},$ it suffices to require that:

$$
\mathsf{m}\geq \operatorname{log}q + 7 \mathsf{s}- 2 + 2 \operatorname{log}( \mathsf{s}+ 1 ) + 2 \mathsf{s}\cdot \operatorname{log}\frac{e \cdot \mathsf{m}}{\mathsf{s}}
$$

For log $q=256$ and $\mathsf{s}=60,$ the minimum number of OTs needed for which the above holds is 1405 (in which case, the right-hand side is 1404.79).

## 6 The ECDSA Two-Party Signing Protocol

In this section, we present optimizations of [DKLs24] for the two-party setting.
These include removing steps that are unnecessary in the two-party setting, and separating the protocol into an offline phase and an online phase.

<a id="section-6-1-the-functionality"></a>

### 6.1 The Functionality

We show that the protocol implements the following functionality, assuming that the inputs of the parties are $\mathsf{valid}\mathsf{and}\mathsf{consistent}[$ Lin24]:

1. The two parties hold the joint public keys $(\mathsf{pk},\mathsf{pk}_{0},\mathsf{pk}_{1})$ such that $\mathsf{pk}=\mathsf{pk}_{0}+\mathsf{pk}_{1}.$

2. Each party $P_{i}$ holds its secret key $\mathsf{sk}_{i}.$ It holds that $\mathsf{sk}_{i}\cdot G=\mathsf{pk}_{i}$ (and thus, $(\mathsf{sk}_{0}+\mathsf{sk}_{1})\cdot G=\mathsf{pk}).$

The functionality is as follows:

Functionality $6.1:\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{Sign}}^{\mathsf{ECDSA}}$

- Upon receiving $(\mathsf{Sign},\mathsf{sid},m,\mathsf{pk},\mathsf{pk}_{0},\mathsf{pk}_{1};\mathsf{sk}_{i})$ from $P_{i}$ for the two parties, verify that all public-inputs $(\mathsf{Sign},\mathsf{sid},m,\mathsf{pk},\mathsf{pk}_{0},\mathsf{pk}_{1})$ are valid and consistent as above $(\mathsf{pk}=\mathsf{pk}_{0}+\mathsf{pk}_{1},$ and $\mathsf{sk}_{i}\cdot G=$ $\mathsf{pk}_{i}).$ If not, then do nothing;

- Otherwise, set $\mathsf{sk}=\mathsf{sk}_{0}+\mathsf{sk}_{1},$ choose a random $r\leftarrow \mathsf{Scalar}$ and compute $R=r\cdot G,$ and let $r^{\mathsf{x}}$ be the $x$-coordinate of $R.$ Compute

$s=r^{-1}\cdot (\mathsf{H}(m)+\mathsf{sk}\cdot r^{\mathsf{x}}),$

and set $\sigma =(r^{\mathsf{x}},s).$ Send $(\mathsf{sid},\mathsf{pk},\mathsf{pk}_{0},\mathsf{pk}_{1},m,\sigma )$ to the ideal adversary.
When the adversary sends $\mathsf{OK},$ send the output to the honest party as well.

We remark that ECDSA restarts when $r^{\mathsf{x}}=0,$ or $s=0;$ this happens by a negligible probability by the functionality, and we can assume that the functionality aborts if this is the case.

<a id="section-6-2-the-protocol"></a>

### 6.2 The Protocol

We provide a protocol that implements Functionality 6.1 assuming commitment $(F_{\mathsf{com}},$ Functionality 3.3) and two instances of vector OLE $(\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{RVOLE}},$ Functionality 4.1), one instance in which $P_{0}$ is the sender and $P_{1}$ is the receiver, and one for the opposite direction.
The protocol provides a few differences from that of [DKLs24]:

- Random VOLE and pre-processing: The most significant shift is the separation of the protocol into a pre-processing phase using Random VOLE (RVOLE) instead of standard VOLE. Whereas the protocol of [DKLs24] requires inputs (of the Sender) to be known at the time of the VOLE call, this protocol allows parties to generate random correlations $(\widetilde{r},\widetilde{sk},\beta )$ ahead of time.
During the signing phase, the parties simply “fix” these random values to their actual secrets using the offsets $\gamma _{r_{i}}$ and $\gamma _{\mathsf{sk}_{i}}.$

- No corrections: Since we deal with two parties, there is no need to correlate between the $n-1$ different masking inputs of the Receiver to one global masking.

- No $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{zero}}:$ In the two-party setting, the $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{zero}}$ functionality used in [DKLs24] to mask input shares of $\mathsf{sk}$ is unnecessary.

We proceed to the full protocol:

Protocol 6.2: Implementing Functionality 6.1 in the $(F_{\mathsf{com}},\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{RVOLE}})$-hybrid model

Input pre-processing: A shared $\mathsf{sid}.$

Hybrid model (sub-protocols): The parties run $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{RVOLE}}$ twice, once when $P_{0}$ is the sender, and once when $P_{1}$ is the sender.The analysis is in the hybrid model, while the protocol here assumes the implementation given in Protocol 4.2, and follows its structure.

1. First round $P_{i}$ for $i\in \{0,1\}:$

(a) $P_{i}$ samples a secret nonce $r_{i}\leftarrow \mathsf{Scalar}$ and computes $R_{i}:=r_{i}\cdot G.$

(b) $P_{i}$ commits to $R_{i}:$ it sends $(\mathsf{commit},\mathsf{sid}\Vert \mathtt{com},i,R_{i})$ to $F_{\mathsf{com}}$ (Functionality 3.3). (c) Invoke $m_{1}^{i}=\mathsf{VOLE}.\mathsf{R}.Round1(\mathsf{sid}\Vert \mathtt{VOLE}\Vert 1-i).$

(d) Message: Send to $P_{1-i}$ the message: $m_{1}^{i}$ together with the commitment.

2. Second round $P_{i}$ for $i\in \{0,1\}:$

(a) Receive $(\mathsf{receipt},\mathsf{sid}\Vert \mathtt{com},1-i)$ from $F_{\mathsf{com}}.$ If not, abort.

(b) $P_{i}$ computes $(m_{2}^{i},\widetilde{r}_{i},\widetilde{\mathsf{sk}}_{i},$ $[[\widetilde{r}_{i}\cdot \beta _{1-i}]]_{i},[[\widetilde{\mathsf{sk}}_{i}\cdot \beta _{1-i}]]_{i})=\mathsf{VOLE}.\mathsf{S}.Round2(\mathsf{sid}\Vert \mathtt{VOLE}\Vert i,m_{1}^{1-i}).$ (c) Message: Send to $P_{1-i}$ the message $m_{2}^{i}.$

3. Compute Pre-processing:

(a) $P_{i}$ computes $(\beta _{i},[[\widetilde{r}_{1-i}\cdot \beta _{i}]]_{i},[[\widetilde{\mathsf{sk}}_{1-i}\cdot \beta _{i}]]_{i})=\mathsf{VOLE}.\mathsf{R}.\mathsf{ComputeOutput}(\mathsf{sid}\Vert \mathtt{VOLE}\Vert 1-i,m_{2}^{1-i}).$ (b) Output: Store as a secret state:

$$
( \beta_{i}, \lbrack\!\lbrack  \widetilde{r}_{1 - i}\cdot \beta_{i}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{i}, \lbrack\!\lbrack  \widetilde{\mathsf{sk}}_{1 - i}\cdot \beta_{i}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{i}, \quad \widetilde{r}_{i}, \widetilde{\mathsf{sk}}_{i}, \quad \lbrack\!\lbrack  \widetilde{r}_{i}\cdot \beta_{1 - i}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{i}, \lbrack\!\lbrack  \widetilde{\mathsf{sk}}_{i}\cdot \beta_{1 - i}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{i}, \quad r_{i}) .
$$

(as well as the commitment/decommitment information in the plain model).

Note that at this point, it holds that:

$$
\begin{aligned}{}&{{}\lbrack\!\lbrack  \widetilde{r}_{1}\cdot \beta_{0}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{0}+ \lbrack\!\lbrack  \widetilde{r}_{1}\cdot \beta_{0}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{1}= \widetilde{r}_{1}\cdot \beta_{0}, \qquad}&{\qquad}&{{}\lbrack\!\lbrack  \widetilde{\mathsf{sk}}_{1}\cdot \beta_{0}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{0}+ \lbrack\!\lbrack  \widetilde{\mathsf{sk}}_{1}\cdot \beta_{0}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{1}= \widetilde{\mathsf{sk}}_{1}\cdot \beta_{0},}\\{}&{{}\lbrack\!\lbrack  \widetilde{r}_{0}\cdot \beta_{1}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{0}+ \lbrack\!\lbrack  \widetilde{r}_{0}\cdot \beta_{1}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{1}= \widetilde{r}_{0}\cdot \beta_{1}, \qquad}&{\qquad}&{{}\lbrack\!\lbrack  \widetilde{\mathsf{sk}}_{0}\cdot \beta_{1}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{0}+ \lbrack\!\lbrack  \widetilde{\mathsf{sk}}_{0}\cdot \beta_{1}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{1}= \widetilde{\mathsf{sk}}_{0}\cdot \beta_{1}.}\\ \end{aligned}
$$

Signing phase: The input of both parties is the joint public key $\mathsf{pk},$ the individual public keys $(\mathsf{pk}_{0},\mathsf{pk}_{1})$ such that $\mathsf{pk}=\mathsf{pk}_{0}+\mathsf{pk}_{1}$ (abort if this is not the case), and the message $m\in \{0,1\}^{\ast},$ as well as descriptions of $\mathsf{Scalar},\mathbb{G}.$ The private input of $P_{0}$ is its share $\mathsf{sk}_{0},$ and the private input of $P_{1}$ is its share $\mathsf{sk}_{1}.$ Both parties also hold their respective private state from the pre-processing phase.

1. First round $P_{i}$ for $i\in \{0,1\}:$

(a) Compute the fixes of the RVOLE:

$$
\begin{array}{r c l}{\gamma_{r_{i}}}&{=}&{r_{i}- \widetilde{r}_{i}}\\{\gamma_{\mathsf{sk}_{i}}}&{=}&{\mathsf{sk}_{i}- \widetilde{\mathsf{sk}}_{i}.}\\ \end{array}
$$

(b) Re-define the shares:

$$
\begin{array}{r c l}{[  [{r_{i}\cdot \beta_{1 - i}}]  ]_{i}}&{: =}&{[  [{\widetilde{r}_{i}\cdot \beta_{1 - i}}]  ]_{i}}\\{[  [{\mathsf{sk}_{i}\cdot \beta_{1 - i}}]  ]_{i}}&{: =}&{[  [{\widetilde{\mathsf{sk}}_{i}\cdot \beta_{1 - i}}]  ]_{i}.}\\ \end{array}
$$

(c) Compute:

$$
\begin{array}{r c l}{\Gamma_{r_{i}}}&{=}&{\lbrack\!\lbrack  \mathbb{1}_{i}\cdot \beta_{1 - i}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{i}\cdot G ,}\\{\Gamma_{\mathsf{sk}_{i}}}&{=}&{\lbrack\!\lbrack  \mathsf{sk}_{i}\cdot \beta_{1 - i}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{i}\cdot G .}\\ \end{array}
$$

(d) It calls $(\mathsf{decommit},\mathsf{sid}\Vert \mathtt{com},i)$ to $F_{\mathsf{com}}$ (Functionality 3.3).

(e) Message: It sends to $P_{1-i}$ the message

$$
m_{2}= ( \Gamma_{r_{i}}, \Gamma_{\mathsf{sk}_{i}}, \gamma_{r_{i}}, \gamma_{\mathsf{sk}_{i}}) .
$$

and the decommitment information.

2. Second round $P_{i}:$

(a) $P_{i}$ obtains the message $(\Gamma _{r_{1-i}},\Gamma _{\mathsf{sk}_{1-i}},\gamma _{r_{1-i}},\gamma _{\mathsf{sk}_{1-i}}).$

(b) Given the pre-computed values $[[\widetilde{r}_{1-i}\cdot \beta _{i}]]_{i},[[\widetilde{\mathsf{sk}}_{1-i}\cdot \beta _{i}]]_{i},\beta _{i},$ define the shifted shares:

$$
\begin{aligned}{\lbrack\!\lbrack  r_{1 - i}\cdot \beta_{i}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{i}}&{{}= \gamma_{r_{1 - i}}\cdot \beta_{i}+ \lbrack\!\lbrack  \widetilde{r}_{1 - i}\cdot \beta_{i}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{i},}\\{\lbrack\!\lbrack  \mathsf{sk}_{1 - i}\cdot \beta_{i}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{i}}&{{}= \gamma_{\mathsf{sk}_{1 - i}}\cdot \beta_{i}+ \lbrack\!\lbrack  \widetilde{\mathsf{sk}}_{1 - i}\cdot \beta_{i}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{i}.}\\ \end{aligned}
$$

(c) $P_{i}$ receives $(\mathsf{decommit},\mathsf{sid}\Vert \mathtt{com},1-i,R_{1-i})$ from $F_{\mathsf{com}}.$

(d) It verifies that all of the following hold:

$i.R_{1-i}$ is a point on the curve.

ii. $\Gamma _{r_{1-i}},\Gamma _{\mathsf{sk}_{1-i}}$ are points on the curve.
Otherwise, $\mathsf{abort}.$

(e) Verify the shifted shares

$$
\begin{aligned}{\lbrack\!\lbrack  r_{1 - i}\cdot \beta_{i}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{i}\cdot G}&{{}= \beta_{i}\cdot R_{1 - i}- \Gamma_{r_{1 - i}},}\\{\lbrack\!\lbrack  \mathsf{sk}_{1 - i}\cdot \beta_{i}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{i}\cdot G}&{{}= \beta_{i}\cdot \mathsf{pk}_{1 - i}- \Gamma_{\mathsf{sk}_{1 - i}}.}\\ \end{aligned}
$$

if any of the equations do not hold, abort.

(f) Compute:

$$
R=R_0+R_1,
$$

and let $r^{\mathsf{x}}$ be the $x$-coordinate of $R.$

(g) Compute:

$$
\begin{array}{r c l}{u_{i}}&{=}&{r_{i}\cdot \beta_{i}+ \lbrack\!\lbrack  r_{i}\cdot \beta_{1 - i}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{i}+ \lbrack\!\lbrack  r_{1 - i}\cdot \beta_{i}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{i},}\\{v_{i}}&{=}&{\mathsf{sk}_{i}\cdot \beta_{i}+ \lbrack\!\lbrack  \mathsf{sk}_{i}\cdot \beta_{1 - i}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{i}+ \lbrack\!\lbrack  \mathsf{sk}_{1 - i}\cdot \beta_{i}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{i},}\\{w_{i}}&{=}&{\mathsf{H}( m ) \cdot \beta_{i}+ r^{\mathsf{x}}\cdot v_{i}.}\\ \end{array}
$$

(h) Message: Send

$m_{3}=(u_{i},w_{i}).$

3. Compute output $P_{i}:$

(a) $P_{i}$ receives $(u_{1-i},w_{1-i}).$

(b) If $R$ is invalid, or $r^{\mathsf{x}}=0,$ or $u=u_{0}+u_{1}=0,$ then $\mathsf{abort}$ and discard the preprocessing state.

(c) $P_{i}$ computes:

$w=w_{0}+w_{1},$ $u=u_{0}+u_{1},$ $s=w/u,$

and $\mathsf{abort}$ if $s=0.$ Otherwise, set $\sigma =(r^{\mathsf{x}},s).$

(d) Verify that $\mathsf{Vrfy}_{\mathsf{pk}}(m,\sigma )=1.$ If not, $\mathsf{abort}.$

(e) Output: $\sigma .$

In all cases, after successful output or any abort, discard the preprocessing state.

We prove the following theorem in Appendix C: Theorem 6.3. Protocol 6.2 securely realizes $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{Sign}}^{\mathsf{ECDSA}}$ (Functionality 6.1) in the $(F_{\mathsf{com}},\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{RVOLE}})-$ hybrid model (Functionalities 3.3, 4.1).

## 7 Evaluation and Efficiency

We evaluate the communication complexity of the different VOLE variants, as well as the communication cost of the signing protocol.

The VOLE protocol.
Independently of the concrete implementation of the OT functionality, the parties must transmit the matrix $\mathbf{Q}\in \mathsf{Scalar}^{\mathsf{m}\times (\ell +1)}.$ Recall that each element of $\mathsf{Scalar}$ is represented using 256 bits (ignoring serialization overhead). Table 1 summarizes the communication cost of each VOLE variant, excluding the additional communication incurred by the OTs themselves.

In our implementation, the total bandwidth is approximately doubled once the OT communication is included.
Furthermore, the values reported in the table correspond to the amount of data sent by each party.
Since the protocol is symmetric, each party also receives a similar amount of communication.

State size.
In contrast to the communication cost of the VOLE preprocessing, the state maintained by each party after preprocessing is relatively small.
Concretely, each party stores the following values:

$$
\left( \beta_{i}, \lbrack\!\lbrack  \widetilde{r}_{1 - i}\cdot \beta_{i}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{i}, \lbrack\!\lbrack  \widetilde{\mathsf{sk}}_{1 - i}\cdot \beta_{i}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{i}, \widetilde{r}_{i}, \widetilde{\mathsf{sk}}_{i}, \lbrack\!\lbrack  \widetilde{r}_{i}\cdot \beta_{1 - i}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{i}, \lbrack\!\lbrack  \widetilde{\mathsf{sk}}_{i}\cdot \beta_{1 - i}\rbrack\!\rbrack_{i}, r_{i}\right) .
$$

For Secp256k1, overall, this corresponds to eight field elements, each represented using 256 bits.
In addition, the parties store the decommitment information $(\kappa$ bits) and the commitment to $R_{1-i}$ $(2\kappa$ bits). Hence, the total state size is

$$
8 \cdot 256 + 256 + 128 = 2432{\mathrm{~ b i t s}},
$$

which is approximately 304 bytes (roughly $0.$ 3KB).

Communication in the signing phase.
The signing protocol consists of two rounds.

1. In the first round, each party opens its commitment by sending $R_{i}$ together with the corresponding decommitment information.
In addition, it sends

$(\Gamma _{r_{i}},\Gamma _{\mathsf{sk}_{i}},\gamma _{r_{i}},\gamma _{\mathsf{sk}_{i}}).$

This corresponds to three group elements (257 bits each), two scalars (256 bits each), and decommitment information (128 bits), for a total of approximately 1400 bits.

2. In the second round, each party sends $(u_{i},w_{i}),$ consisting of two scalars, for a total of 512 bits.

Overall, each party sends approximately 1900 bits during the signing phase, corresponding to roughly $0.$ 2KB.

<a id="section-a-experiment-on-small-parameters"></a>

## $\mathbf{A}$ Experiment on Small Parameters

Our analysis addresses that one cannot apply the leftover-hash lemma directly in the original protocol of [DKLs24]. However, this does not rule out the possibility that the parameter choice given in [DKLs24] is correct, but requires some new proof.

In this section, we investigate whether the parameter choice in [DKLs24] is correct.
We show that for small parameters, it does not achieve the intended security level.
That is, we show that for a random $\mathbf{g}\in \mathsf{Scalar}^{\mathsf{m}},$ there exists some constraint $\mathbf{c},$ which defines a distribution $W_{\mathbf{c}}$ such that $\mathsf{SD}(\mathbf{g}(W_{\mathbf{c}}),U_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}})>2^{-\mathsf{s}}.$

Towards that end, we implemented a small script in Python that runs over all possibilities.
Of course, such code takes a long time to run (quantifying over all possible $\mathbf{g}s),$ and so we can run it over small parameters only.
We consider:

1. $\mathbb{Z}_{q}=\{0,...,6\},i.e.,q=7.$ This implies that log $q=3.$

2. We took $\mathsf{s}=2.$

3. While in our security game, the adversary is allowed to output $\mathsf{s}$ constraints, we run with fixing just a single constraint.

4. Quantifying over all $\mathbf{g},$ we computed whether there exists a (single) constraint $\mathbf{c}$ for which $\mathsf{SD}(\mathbf{g}(W_{\mathbf{c}}),U_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}})>2^{-\mathsf{s}},$ where $U_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}}$ is the uniform distribution over $\mathbb{Z}_{q}.$

5. We took the parameters suggested by [DKLs24]. That is, $\mathsf{m}=$ log $q+2\mathsf{s}=3+2\cdot 2=7.$

6. For each $\mathbf{g},$ we say it is “bad” if there exists $\mathbf{c}$ for which $\mathsf{SD}(\mathbf{g}(W_{\mathbf{c}}),U_{\mathbb{Z}_{q}})>2^{-\mathsf{s}},$ and “good” otherwise.

7. For that set of parameters, the number of bad $\mathbf{g}$ is 220087, out of 823543 possible functions.
That is, more than 26% of the possible functions have a bad set of constraints that result in a larger statistical distance than required.

8. We remark that this is when the adversary is allowed to provide a single constraint; In the analysis, it is allowed to provide up to $\mathsf{s}$ constraints.

The above shows that the bounds provided in [DKLs24] are too liberal.

Claim B.8. Assuming that log $q\geq \kappa +2$ log $\mathsf{m},$ for every $\mathbf{A}:$

$$

where recall that $J=\{j\in [\mathsf{m}]|(j,1)\in \mathsf{constraints}\}.$ Assuming both hybrids do not abort, recall that $u_{j}-u_{j}^{\ast }=w_{j}\cdot \Delta _{j}.$ We have:

1. For every $j\in [\mathsf{m}]$ such that $\Delta _{j}=0,i.e.,\langle \mathbf{A}_{j},\chi \rangle =v,$ it must hold that $\alpha _{j,i}=\alpha _{i}.$ This holds from the fact that $|S|=1,$ and recall that both hybrids abort if $|S|\not =1.$ As such, $(\alpha _{j,i}-\alpha _{i})=0,$ and we can ignore $j$ in the summation.

2. For every $j\in [\mathsf{m}]$ such that $\Delta _{j}\not =0,$ there are two cases, conditioned on no abort: (a) If $u_{j}^{\ast}=u_{j}$ then it must hold that $w_{j}=0,$ and we can ignore $j$ in the summation. (b) If $u_{j}^{\ast }\not =u_{j}$ and no abort, then $(j,1)\in \mathsf{constraints}.$ Therefore, for every $\mathbf{w}$ in the support, for which the hybrids do not abort:

$$
g_{j}\cdot ( \alpha_{j , i}- \alpha_{i}) = g_{j}\cdot w_{j}( \alpha_{j , i}- \alpha_{i}) .
$$

Claim B.11. Hyb<sup>7</sup> and Hyb<sup>8</sup> are identically distributed.

Proof.
The difference is just syntactic.

In Hyb $S7$ we sample $\mathbf{w}$ uniformly at random from $\{0,1\}^{\mathsf{m}}.$ Then:

1. We check whether $\mathbf{w}$ is consistent with $\mathsf{constraints}.$ For a random $\mathbf{w},$ this occurs with probability $2^{-|\mathsf{constraints}|}.$

2. If it is not consistent (with probability $1-2^{-|\mathsf{constraints}|})$ we abort.

3. Otherwise, we proceed.
Note that this implies that for every constraint $(w_{j}=\rho )\in \mathsf{constraints}$ it must hold that $w_{j}=\rho .$

In Hyb $S8$ we instead choose whether to abort with probability $1-2^{-|\mathsf{constraints}|}.$ Then, if not aborted, we proceed with picking $\mathbf{w}^{\ast}$ such that it must agree with $\mathsf{constraints}.$ This means fixing all the indices that there are constraints, and choosing the rest uniformly at random.

Claim B.12. Hyb $S8$ and Hyb $S9$ are $2^{-\mathsf{s}}$ statistically-close.

Proof.
The difference between the two hybrids is as follows.
If $|\mathsf{constraints}|\geq \mathsf{s},$ then Hyb $S9$ always aborts.
On the other hand, Hyb $S8$ samples $\mathbf{w}^{\ast}$ and verifies whether $\mathbf{w}^{\ast}$ is consistent with $\mathsf{constraints},$ and if not, it aborts.
If $|\mathsf{constraints}|\geq \mathsf{s}$ then the probability that Hyb $S8$ does not abort is smaller than $2^{-\mathsf{s}}.$ Thus, the difference between the two hybrids is at most $2^{-\mathsf{s}}.$

Claim B.13. Hyb $S9$ and Hyb $S10$ are $2^{-\mathsf{s}}$-statistically close.

Proof.
The only difference between the two hybrids is in the way $b$ is defined.

- In HybS9, the adversary first outputs its messages, which define a set of constraints $\mathsf{constraints}$ on $\mathbf{w}.$ The choice whether to abort is determined by the size of $\mathsf{constraints}.$ The simulator picks $\mathbf{g}$ uniformly at random, and $\mathbf{w}$ uniformly at random (under the constraint that it is consistent with $\mathsf{constraints}),$ and sets $b=\langle \mathbf{g},\mathbf{w}\rangle .$ The value $b$ is then used to determine $r_{1},...,r_{\ell }$ where each $r_{i}=\alpha _{i}\cdot b-t_{i}.$

- In HybS10, the adversary also output a set of $\mathsf{constraints}.$ The choice whether to abort is determined by the size of $\mathsf{constraints}.$ Conditioning on no abort, the trusted party picks $b$ uniformly at random in $\mathsf{Scalar},$ and also sets $r_{i}=\alpha _{i}\cdot b-t_{i}$ for every $i\in [\ell ].$

The two hybrids are $2^{-\mathsf{s}}-$ statistically close by Claim 5.3.

From combining all hybrids, we obtain that the distinguishing probability between the real and ideal is bounded by:

$$
( 2^{- \mathsf{m}}+ 2^{- \kappa}) + 2^{- \mathsf{s}}+ 2^{- \mathsf{s}}.
$$

### B.2 Variant II: Compressing Rounds – Security Analysis for Protocol 4.2

We now perform a Fiat-Shamir-like transformation to reduce the number of rounds.
We perform the following modifications, which bring us back to Protocol 4.2:

1. Instead of picking $\chi$ by the receiver, it is computed as $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{ROM}}^{\mathsf{Scalar}^{\ell }}(\mathsf{sid},\mathtt{chi},\mathbf{Q}).$ This eliminates round 3 in the extended protocol, and now the sender can compute rounds 2,3,4 at once. 2. Next, the sender computes $H_{u}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{ROM}}^{2\kappa}(\mathsf{sid},\mathtt{u},\mathbf{u})$ and sends $H_{u}$ instead of $\mathbf{u}$ in its entirety. 3. Finally, the sender derives $\mathbf{g}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{ROM}}^{\mathsf{Scalar}^{\mathsf{m}}}(\mathsf{sid},\mathtt{gadget},m_{2})=\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{ROM}}^{\mathsf{Scalar}^{\mathsf{m}}}(\mathsf{sid},\mathtt{gadget},(\mathbf{Q},H_{u},v))$ instead of receiving it from the receiver.
This eliminates round 5 of the extended protocol, and now the sender can compute rounds 2–6 at once.

We now show how to modify the simulator for each one of those modifications.

Changes in the simulation – corrupted Sender.
We follow the same steps of Simulator B.3, with the following modifications:

1. In Step 5, instead of picking $(\chi _{1},...,\chi _{\ell })$ uniformly at random by the simulator, the simulator simply computes $(\chi _{1},...,\chi _{\ell })=\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{ROM}}^{\mathsf{Scalar}^{\ell }}(\mathsf{sid},\mathtt{chi},\mathbf{Q})$ as the honest Receiver in the real.
Thus, $\chi$ is well-defined at the same point in the simulation as previously.

2. In Step 6, instead of receiving $(\mathbf{u},v)$ from the adversary, the adversary sends just $(H_{u},v).$ The simulator then scans the random oracle queries that the adversary makes and looks for a single query $(\mathsf{sid},\mathtt{u},\mathbf{u})$ that results in $H_{u}.$ If there is no unique query, it aborts.
It then continues while using this unique $\mathbf{u}$ as if the adversary sent it explicitly, and $\mathbf{u}$ is well-defined at the same point in the simulation as previously.

3. In Step 9, instead of sampling $\mathbf{g}$ uniformly at random in $\mathsf{Scalar}^{\mathsf{m}},$ compute: $\mathbf{g}=\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{ROM}}^{\mathsf{Scalar}^{\mathsf{m}}}(\mathsf{sid},$ $\mathtt{gadget},m_{2}),$ where $m_{2}=(\mathbf{Q},H_{u},v).$ Note that the simulator already has those messages.
Thus, $\mathbf{g}$ is well-defined at the same point as previously.

We then show how the above changes affect the simulation.

Picking $\chi$ by the random oracle: For this modification, the simulation fails with probability at most $(Q+1)\cdot 2^{-\kappa},$ where $Q$ bounds the number of queries that the adversary makes to the random oracle.

Specifically, in the $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{RSOT}}$-random oracle, the matrices $\mathbf{T}^{0},\mathbf{T}^{1}$ are fixed.
This implies that each $\mathbf{Q}$ uniquely defines $\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{Q}-\mathbf{T}^{0}+\mathbf{T}^{1}.$ The vector $\chi$ is chosen uniformly for each $\mathbf{Q}.$ Claim B.8 shows that the probability that $(\mathbf{A},\chi )$ is “bad” for a random $\chi$ is bounded by $2^{-\kappa}.$ Thus, an adversary that makes at most $Q$ queries to the random oracle succeeds in finding a pair $(\mathbf{A},\chi )$ that is bad is bounded by $(Q+1)\cdot 2^{-\kappa}.$

The property of $\chi$ being “not bad” is used in Claim B.7. Since $\chi$ is not bad with overwhelming probability, $|S|=1,$ and the values $(\alpha _{1},...,\alpha _{\ell })$ are well-defined.

Sending $H_{u}$ instead of u.
This further introduces an error of $(Q^{2}+1)\cdot 2^{-2\kappa}.$ There are two cases to consider: either the simulator does not find a query to the random oracle that results in $H_{u},$ or that it finds more than one: • If no query, then the receiver queries a value $\mathbf{u}^{\prime}$ that the sender did not query.
Since $H_{u}$ is fixed, the probability that $\mathcal{F}_{\mathsf{ROM}}^{2\kappa}(\mathsf{sid},\mathtt{u},\mathbf{u}^{\prime})=H_{u}$ is $2^{-2\kappa}.$

- If there are multiple queries that lead to $H_{u},$ then the adversary has found a collision in the random oracle.
The probability that two specific queries collide is less than $2^{-2\kappa}.$ The probability of finding any collision is bounded by $Q^{2}\cdot 2^{-2\kappa}.$

Picking $\mathbf{g}$ by the random oracle.
This modification leads to increasing the number of OTs from log $q+3\mathsf{s}(\approx 436)$ to log $q+2\cdot \kappa +3\mathsf{s}(\approx 692).$

Specifically, the adversary can now, given a candidate $(\mathbf{Q},H_{u},v),$ after fixing the corresponding extracted $\mathbf{u}$ except for a collision error probability, the adversary can query the random oracle to retrieve $\mathbf{g}$ and decide whether to proceed with that $\mathbf{g}$ or not.
Each $(\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{u},v)$ defines a set of constraints $\mathbf{c}.$ For each such quadruple, there is a unique $\mathbf{g}$ that will be chosen by the random oracle.
The adversary can try many different quadruples.
Claim 5.5 captures exactly this scenario and shows that when $\mathsf{m}\geq$ log $q+3\mathsf{s}+2\kappa ,$ the statistical distance between $\langle \mathbf{g},\mathbf{w}\rangle$ and the uniform distribution over $\mathsf{Scalar}$ is at most $Q\cdot 2^{-\kappa}+2^{-\mathsf{s}},$ for an adversary that makes $Q$ queries to the random oracle.

Changes in the simulation – corrupted Receiver.
